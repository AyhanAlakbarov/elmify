# Evaluating Web Search APIs for a URL-Discovery-Only Architecture

**Scope note:** All terms quotes below were read from each provider's own legal pages on **August 29, 2026**. The product's model is: use search **only to discover URLs**, then fetch each URL independently from the origin server, snapshot the fetched page, and cite that snapshot — never the ranked list, titles, or snippets.

## TL;DR
- **Yes — several providers' terms permit retaining a bare URL, and one grants it in writing.** Mojeek explicitly sells "Storage Rights" on its Business and Enterprise plans; You.com and Tavily are effectively silent on (and therefore do not prohibit) retaining API output; Common Crawl is open data. This is the single most important finding: URL-only retention is achievable within terms, which means the architecture does not need to change.
- **The real blockers are not "caching" clauses but "no database / no competing index / must-fetch-live" clauses.** Google's API terms forbid building databases or permanent copies; Brave forbids retaining "Search Results" beyond transient use unless you buy a storage-rights plan; Exa restricts retaining information "obtained from or through" its Services. SERP resellers (Serper, SerpApi) inherit Google's terms and its active anti-scraping litigation.
- **Terms-first ranking:** Mojeek (explicit storage grant) > Tavily (output not restricted) > Brave (clean but needs paid storage plan) > Exa/You.com > Serper/SerpApi (terms-risky). Bing/Azure is retired; Google CSE is closing; DuckDuckGo and Startpage offer no lawful official API.

## Question 0 — Does storing only the URL fall inside or outside the retention restriction?

The product retains one thing from the API: the URL string. It does not store the snippet, title, ranking, or result set. Whether that is inside or outside each provider's retention restriction turns on (a) how "results/content" is defined and (b) whether the clause bars *storage* or only *display/derived databases*.

**Providers that clearly permit URL retention:**

- **Mojeek — YES, in writing.** Its Web Search API page states: *"You can store results on Business plan and optionally on the Enterprise plan. For other plans, you may store the results for 1 hour to enable caching."* (mojeek.com/services/search/web-search-api/, read Aug 29 2026). Since storing the whole result is permitted on Business+, storing only the URL is *a fortiori* permitted. The same page adds an unusually broad reuse grant: *"You can combine search results from Mojeek with those from other search engines, search services or any other data source. You can use the data with other data sources for research, reporting, AI or whatever you wish."* This is the strongest positive finding in the run.
- **You.com — SILENT (report as silence, not permission).** Neither its Terms (you.com/terms, last revised Aug 27 2024) nor its Master Services Agreement (you.com/msa, v.121525 / Dec 15 2025) contains any clause prohibiting caching, storing, or retaining API output. There is no anti-cache clause and therefore no provision addressing URL-only retention either way. The binding restrictions are on *extraction* (§3.5: *"automatically or programmatically extracting data or Output"*) and *competition* (*"developing any products or services that compete with our Services, including the development or training of any artificial intelligence, machine learning algorithms or large language models"*). Neither reaches a customer storing URLs it legitimately received via the API.
- **Tavily — SILENT / permissive.** Tavily's Platform Terms (tavily.com/terms, last updated May 4 2026) define "Output" and then state: *"For clarity, the term 'Services' does not include Output."* The §3.2 use restrictions (no reverse engineering, no reselling, no competing product) attach to the "Services," not to Output. There is no clause barring storage of Output. Tavily even ships a **Map** endpoint whose stated purpose is to "Discover URLs across any website" — i.e., URL discovery is a first-class use.
- **Common Crawl — YES (open data).** The dataset is offered under permissive terms; CC staff confirm it is distributed as open data with attribution suggested, though "all of the web content in our datasets is copyrighted by people other than us." Retaining URLs from the CDX index is unrestricted.

**Providers where URL-only retention is restricted or unclear:**

- **Brave — NO on the standard plan; storage requires a paid tier.** Definitions: *"'Search Results' means any outputs from calls made to the API, which may consist of (i) internet search results…"* — a URL is such an output. §3(b): *"Customer shall not… (i) store, cache, or create a database of Search Results, in whole or in part, other than transient storage required for operation of Customer Applications…"* (api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/resources/terms-of-service, "Last Updated: 11 February 2026"). Retaining a URL to fetch and cite later exceeds "transient storage required for operation." Brave confirms in its FAQ: *"If you would like to store the API results in part or whole… you will need to subscribe to a plan that explicitly grants storage rights."* So URL-only retention is **No** on the base plan and **Yes** only on a storage-rights plan.
- **Exa — Unclear / No under the standard ToS.** §4.2(a) bars: *"download, modify, copy, distribute… or offer for sale any information contained on, or obtained from or through, the Services, except for temporary files that are automatically cached by your web browser…"* (exa.ai Terms of Service PDF). A persistently stored URL is "information obtained… through the Services" and is not a browser cache. Exa's marketed "Zero Data Retention" concerns **Exa's** retention of the customer's queries, not the customer's right to retain results — a distinction that must not be conflated. Clean retention likely needs an enterprise/DPA arrangement. §4.2(f) also forbids using output "to develop any competitive product or service."
- **Google (APIs ToS, inherited by CSE/JSON API and by SERP resellers) — NO.** *"you will not… Scrape, build databases, or otherwise create permanent copies of such content, or keep cached copies longer than permitted by the cache header"* (developers.google.com/terms). A persistent list of discovered URLs is a database/permanent copy of content. The Programmable Search Engine ToS separately forbids reordering/modifying results: *"You shall not… edit, modify, truncate, filter or change the order of the information contained in any Results."*

**Bottom line for Q0:** A bare URL is a genuinely thin claim, and the product's snapshot is of the *origin's* content obtained independently — not the API's result payload. But "thin" is not "permitted." The clean, defensible paths are (1) Mojeek's written storage grant, (2) You.com/Tavily's silence-plus-no-competing-use, (3) Common Crawl's open data, or (4) a Brave/Exa enterprise storage plan.

## Question 2 — Fitness for the actual job (site-scoped, funding-shaped, multilingual)

**site: reliability.** All index-owning providers support host confinement. Brave and You.com expose a genuine `site:` operator; Exa and Tavily use structured `include_domains` (Exa additionally supports **path prefixes and subdomain wildcards like `*.substack.com`**, which is the most precise tool for confining to a university's estate). Serper/SerpApi pass `site:` straight to Google, giving the most literal host confinement — at the cost of Google's terms.

**University-subdomain coverage — the decisive axis.** Funded graduate schools frequently live on a separate subdomain (e.g., `gradschool.uni.edu`, `stipendien.uni.de`) that a site's own sitemap does not enumerate — which is exactly why search is wanted. Here the reseller-of-Google options (Serper, SerpApi) and Google's own CSE have the deepest subdomain coverage, because Google crawls those subdomains aggressively. Brave and You.com's independent indexes are broad but will miss some obscure funded-school subdomains. Exa's subdomain-wildcard filter is powerful but only as complete as Exa's crawl. Mojeek's smaller index is the weakest on long-tail university subdomains. **Common Crawl is a poor substitute here specifically:** it samples rather than deep-crawls ("we do not aim to have a complete copy of any web site"), so a rarely-linked funded-school subdomain is exactly what it is most likely to miss.

**Non-English retrievability.** The query set spans German, Korean, Turkish, Chinese, French, and Hungarian pages. Two facts matter: (1) coverage of non-English pages in the index, and (2) whether English query terms retrieve them. Google-backed options (Serper/SerpApi/CSE) and Brave have the broadest non-Latin-script coverage (Korean, Chinese) and cross-lingual matching. Exa's neural search matches by meaning and takes language filters, helping for English-query→local-page retrieval, but is strongest on English content. **Mojeek's coverage is its weak point** — its own materials emphasise English/French/German, so Korean, Turkish, Chinese, and Hungarian funding pages are a real risk. For non-English pages, best practice is to issue queries in the local language rather than relying on English terms.

## Question 3 — Alternatives to a commercial search API

- **Common Crawl (CDX/URL index).** Genuinely useful and free: the CDX server (index.commoncrawl.org) accepts per-domain and wildcard queries (`url=*.uni.edu/*`) and returns URL lists cheaply, and `cdx_toolkit`/`cdx-index-client` automate this. **Where it fails:** freshness (monthly crawls, no single all-crawl index; you query one monthly index at a time), and *sampling* — it deliberately does not fully crawl any site, so newly-created or thinly-linked funded-school subdomains are frequently absent. Use it as a **supplement** for bulk domain enumeration, not as a substitute for live site: search.
- **Self-hosted search over already-fetched pages.** Replaces the *re-finding* problem (indexing pages you have already snapshotted) but **cannot discover new URLs** you have never fetched. It is a complement downstream of discovery, not a discovery tool.
- **Provider sitemaps and on-site search endpoints.** Where a university publishes a sitemap or a site-search JSON endpoint, this is the cheapest, most authoritative source. **Where it fails:** the funded graduate schools that sit on separate subdomains are exactly what the main sitemap does not list — the precise gap that motivated using web search. Sitemaps are worth harvesting first, then backfilling with search.
- **National/academic crawlable indexes.** There is no general, current, crawlable public index of worldwide university web content that could substitute for site: search across German, Korean, Turkish, Chinese, French, and Hungarian institutions. Common Crawl is the closest open corpus and, as noted, is stale and sampled.

## Recommendations (ranked terms-first, capability-second)

1. **Mojeek Business plan (£3 CPM) as the terms-clean default — pilot immediately, but gate on a multilingual coverage test.** It is the only provider with an explicit written storage grant and an independent index, so retention is unambiguously lawful. Before committing, run a benchmark of ~50 known funded-school pages across all six languages; if Korean/Turkish/Chinese/Hungarian recall is poor (the likely outcome), demote it to English/French/German queries only.
2. **Tavily as the primary capability engine for URL discovery.** Output is contractually excluded from the restricted "Services," it ships a purpose-built **Map** URL-discovery endpoint, supports domain allow-lists and time filters, and is multilingual. Confirm in writing whether/which upstream index it resells (its own terms reference "queries on third-party public web indexes"), and stay clear of the no-competing-product clause.
3. **Brave Search API on a storage-rights plan** if you want the cleanest fully-independent Western index with true `site:` and freshness support. Budget $5/1k and explicitly contract the storage-rights tier — the base plan's transient-only clause is a real blocker.
4. **Exa** where subdomain-precise discovery matters most (its `*.uni.edu` wildcard filter is best-in-class); resolve retention via an enterprise/DPA that overrides §4.2(a), and do not mistake its ZDR (about your queries) for a retention permission.
5. **You.com** as a permissive-by-silence fallback with a large own index; get written confirmation rather than relying on silence before depending on it.
6. **Serper / SerpApi only if Google's terms risk is acceptable.** They offer the best subdomain and non-English coverage and the cheapest queries, and Serper's own ToS even permits retention — but both resell scraped Google results and inherit Google's "no database" rule and its active anti-scraping litigation. In *Google v. SerpApi* (N.D. Cal.), Chief Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers granted SerpApi's motion to dismiss on July 20, 2026; per SerpApi's own release, "the court held that Google failed to state a valid claim under the DMCA" and dismissed — **without leave to refile** — Google's claims to the extent they rest on search results containing no copyrighted content, while giving Google 21 days to refile a narrower complaint only if it can show authorization from the copyright owners of specific results. The litigation is unresolved and the enforcement posture toward resellers remains live. Treat as capability-rich, terms-risky.
7. **Common Crawl** as a free supplement for bulk per-domain URL enumeration; never as the live discovery layer.
8. **Do not build on** Bing/Azure (retired Aug 11 2025), Google CSE/JSON API (closed to new customers; existing customers must migrate by Jan 1 2027, and results cannot be reordered or stored), DuckDuckGo or Startpage (no lawful official API), or Marginalia for this job (index too small and non-English-poor, though its commercial key is terms-clean).

**Staged plan:** (a) Harvest target-host sitemaps and on-site search first (free, authoritative). (b) Backfill discovery with Tavily Map + Brave/You.com `site:` queries in the local language. (c) Run the Mojeek multilingual recall test in parallel; if it passes, consolidate onto Mojeek for the terms guarantee. (d) Keep Common Crawl CDX as a cheap enumeration backstop.

## What would change the recommendation
- **A terms change:** if Brave or Exa added an explicit URL-retention or storage-rights allowance to their standard plans, either would leapfrog Tavily on strength-of-index. Conversely, if Tavily or You.com *added* an anti-cache clause, they would drop sharply.
- **A price change:** Brave already eliminated its card-free free tier (Feb 2026); further increases weaken it versus Mojeek/You.com. Serper's ~$0.30/1k is the floor — a Google enforcement action that shuts resellers would remove the cheapest tier entirely.
- **A new entrant / litigation outcome:** an adverse ruling for the resellers in the ongoing *Google v. SerpApi* proceedings (or a refiled, sustained complaint) would make Serper/SerpApi untenable; a clean, independently-indexed, storage-permissive entrant with strong non-English coverage would displace Mojeek as the terms-clean default.
- **The multilingual test result:** if a single provider demonstrably covers all six languages' university subdomains, capability ceases to be the constraint and the decision collapses back to pure terms — favoring Mojeek or a Brave/Exa storage plan.

## Caveats
- Terms of service change; every quote is dated to its document's stated "last updated" where available (Brave 11 Feb 2026; Tavily 4 May 2026; Common Crawl 7 Mar 2024; You.com Terms 27 Aug 2024 / MSA 15 Dec 2025) and otherwise to the read date of Aug 29 2026. Re-verify before contracting.
- "Silent" is reported as silence, not permission: You.com and Tavily do not prohibit URL retention, but absence of a clause is not an affirmative license — obtain written confirmation for anything load-bearing.
- Reseller inheritance is decisive: Serper and SerpApi's own permissive language does not cure the upstream Google prohibition their data was obtained in breach of.
- Exa's and You.com's exact standard-ToS URLs did not carry a visible "last updated" date at read time; the You.com Terms/MSA dates come from document version strings. Exa's published API spec caps standard `numResults` at 100 (default 10) with one doc note citing a 10,000 maximum; confirm any higher enterprise result cap directly with Exa rather than relying on secondhand "1,000 results" figures.
- You.com Research/Finance API per-tier prices were only partially confirmable on official pages; the $5/1k Web Search and $1/1k Contents figures are confirmed on You.com's own pages, higher research tiers should be verified against you.com/pricing.
