# **Research Report: Master's Admissions CV Evaluation and Information Architecture**

## **Executive Summary**

The premise that a programme-specific tailored curriculum vitae (CV) performs better than a strong generic CV in master's degree admissions is entirely unevidenced in published literature, institutional policy, or empirical studies. An exhaustive analysis of admissions rubrics, evaluation guidelines, and institutional frameworks across European and North American higher education systems reveals a fundamental divergence between corporate recruitment practices and academic admissions.  
In the academic context, the CV operates strictly as an audit document designed to establish chronological continuity, verify eligibility, and index external evidence. Institutional evaluation relies on formalized, credit-weighted rubrics, centralized credential evaluation frameworks under the Lisbon Recognition Convention, and supplementary programmatic forms to assess candidate suitability. The importation of commercial recruitment tactics—such as applicant tracking system (ATS) keyword optimization, aggressive vocabulary tailoring, and unevidenced skill declarations—into the master's admissions space constitutes coaching folklore. Furthermore, applying such tactics carries a documented risk of triggering institutional disqualification for misrepresentation.  
This report establishes the empirical reality of master's CV evaluation, defines the mechanisms by which unfamiliar international institutions are rendered legible to admissions committees, and clearly delineates binding policy from advisory convention and pervasive folklore.

## **Findings Note: Empirical and Procedural Realities of Master's Admissions**

### **Track A: Master's Admissions File Review Mechanisms**

#### **A1. The Evaluative Weight of the CV in Admissions Rubrics**

The CV is universally mandated as a formal requirement in European master's applications, yet it is rarely assigned a direct numerical weight in published admissions rubrics. Admissions committees isolate specific academic and non-cognitive vectors, weighting them through standardized scoring matrices that rely on verified transcripts and motivation letters rather than the CV itself.  
At the Technical University of Munich (TUM), the two-stage aptitude assessment for master's programs explicitly codifies this division. For the M.Sc. in Quantum Science & Technology, the evaluation scale (maximum 120 points for qualification) awards up to 40 points for the bachelor's degree grade and up to 40 points for the letter of motivation1. The motivation letter is heavily scrutinized for specific criteria: the discussion of qualifications relative to program requirements (maximum 20 points) and anticipated goals within the program (maximum 20 points)1. The CV is required to be submitted "without chronological gaps" up to the time of application, yet it carries zero assigned points in the evaluation rubric1. Similarly, the TUM M.Sc. in Biomedical Engineering and Medical Physics awards up to 30 points for academic qualification grades and up to 30 points for a written scientific examination, utilizing the CV solely as a mandatory chronological record2. The M.Sc. in Mathematics in Data Science at TUM utilizes a rigid point system evaluating final grades and discipline-specific course credits against required competencies, again bypassing the CV for direct scoring3.  
In Denmark, the Copenhagen Business School (CBS) explicitly states that the selection process centers on the bachelor's degree, specific passed courses within relevant subject areas, grades, and the personal statement4. The CV is entirely absent from the published selection criteria hierarchy4. CBS policy stresses that the "bachelor degree comes first" and is at the center of the selection process4. In the Netherlands, the TU Delft Aerospace Engineering selection procedure relies on an Academic Aptitude Assessment (40%) and a Selection Exam (60%), requiring the CV merely as a baseline administrative document alongside identity verification5.  
At the University of Oxford, the MSc in Neuroscience assesses candidates based on academic ability, references, personal statements, and interview performance, with no distinct evaluative weight assigned to the CV format or tailoring6. Sweden's national admissions portal, UniversityAdmissions.se, utilizes a centralized "merit rating" system based entirely on the evaluation of previous academic studies and specific eligibility requirements, rather than CV presentation7.  
The table below illustrates the structural displacement of the CV in formal scoring rubrics across diverse European systems:

| Institution / Program | Evaluation Mechanism | Weight Assigned to CV | Weighted Components |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **TUM (Quantum Science)** | 120-Point Aptitude Assessment | 0 Points | Transcript/GPA (40 pts), Motivation Letter (40 pts)1 |
| **TUM (Biomedical Eng.)** | Point-based Assessment | 0 Points | Transcript/GPA (30 pts), Written Exam (30 pts)2 |
| **TU Delft (Aerospace Eng.)** | Formulaic Ranking | 0% | Selection Exam (60%), Academic Aptitude (40%)5 |

| **Swedish HEIs (National)** | Merit Rating Calculation | 0% | Verified academic transcripts and specific course credits7 |

Consequently, the CV functions as an administrative baseline—a chronological map of the applicant's history—rather than a weighted scoring instrument.

#### **A2. File Review: Personnel and Time Allocation**

Master's admissions file reviews are predominantly conducted by academic faculty rather than administrative staff, particularly in highly competitive programs. Oxford's MSc in Neuroscience guarantees that all applications are assessed "by at least 3 members of academic staff with relevant experience and expertise"6. At Oxford colleges, applications are "usually considered by academic staff in the relevant subject area"9. At TUM, the letter of motivation and the overall aptitude assessment are graded by "two members of the board" or the "aptitude assessment committee"1. The reliance on academic faculty reflects the necessity of evaluating nuanced scientific potential, research alignment, and complex non-traditional learning paths as mandated by the Lisbon Recognition Convention11.  
Regarding time allocation, no published evidence was found in the surveyed literature or institutional policies documenting the average time spent reading a master's application file in Europe. While US undergraduate admissions figures (4–12 minutes per file using two-reader models) are widely cited in commercial literature, applying these metrics to European master's admissions is entirely speculative and unevidenced. The presence of multi-stage aptitude assessments, written examinations, and three-person faculty panels suggests a significantly more intensive review process than high-volume undergraduate screening, though specific temporal metrics remain completely unpublished1.

#### **A3. Automated Screening (ATS) in Master's Admissions**

No published evidence was found to support the assertion that European or North American master's degree programs utilize automated Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) to screen CVs by keyword. The claim that university admissions systems parse CVs for programmatic keywords is an aggressive projection of corporate recruitment practices onto academic institutions.  
The European higher education architecture relies on the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS), manual curriculum analysis, and centralized evaluation portals (e.g., uni-assist) to assess eligibility4. For example, TUM compares passed courses directly to core modules via a curricular analysis spreadsheet to determine exact equivalence1. Automated keyword screening of a CV is structurally incompatible with the holistic review mandates, faculty-led evaluations, and strict credit-matching algorithms documented across these institutions3.

#### **A4. Hyperlink Interaction by Reviewers**

There is clear, published evidence that admissions reviewers expect to and will interact with hyperlinks provided in a master's CV, distinguishing academic file review from commercial recruitment behavior where external links are frequently ignored. The Charles University Master in Finance and Data Analytics (MFDA) program publishes explicit advisory guidance: "We ask applicants to include sufficient information (preferably a hypertext link) to identify the schools where they studied, the organizations they worked for, the competitions they participated in, and any other relevant information"15. This confirms that hyperlinks serve as a valued verification mechanism for unfamiliar institutions and academic achievements. Furthermore, official EU documentation for the Europass XML schema includes explicit Website fields, confirming that digital navigation is built into the standard data exchange architecture for European learning mobility16.

### **Track B: Does Programme-Specific Tailoring Change Outcomes?**

This track addresses the primary query: whether adapting a CV to a specific master's programme yields a competitive advantage over a comprehensive generic document.

#### **B1. The Empirical Void**

**No published evidence was found.** There is an absolute void in empirical studies, audit experiments, or published outcome data indicating that a tailored CV outperforms a comprehensive generic CV in master's admissions. Any existing literature demonstrating the efficacy of CV tailoring is isolated to commercial job recruitment and cannot be legitimately transferred to academic admissions. In the academic sphere, rubrics are heavily weighted toward verified transcripts, ECTS equivalencies, and motivation letters1. The premise that an applicant must curate their CV to match a specific master's program rests entirely on coaching folklore.

#### **B2. Admissions Office Statements on Tailoring**

Admissions offices universally mandate the tailoring of the *Statement of Purpose* or *Motivation Letter*, but they do not request CV tailoring. TUM explicitly requires the motivation letter to feature a "Discussion of your qualification with respect to the QST program requirements"1. Oxford demands evidence of "understanding of the proposed area of study" within the personal statement6. Cambridge Judge Business School requires a statement covering "why you have chosen to do the MPhil in Technology Policy"19.  
Conversely, institutional directives regarding the CV demand exhaustiveness rather than programmatic curation. TUM requires a "curriculum vitae without chronological gaps up to the time of your application"1. The centralized German application portal, uni-assist, reinforces this binding rule: "Some universities require a CV without any omissions which is current up to the date of application. List everything you have done up until now. Start with your first school. Do not leave out any period"21. The DAAD (German Academic Exchange Service) similarly demands a "curriculum vitae with no chronological gaps and in tabular form in accordance with EU standards" for its scholarship funding programs22. In European admissions, omission for the sake of "tailoring" is structurally penalized.

#### **B3. Specific Tailoring Tactics: Evidence vs. Folklore**

Given the absence of empirical support and the presence of strict completeness rules, the common CV tailoring tactics heavily promoted by career coaches must be classified as follows:

| Tailoring Tactic | Status in Master's Admissions | Evidentiary Basis |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Reordering existing material** (e.g., placing relevant items first regardless of chronology) | **Folklore / Prohibited** | European standards (like Europass) and DAAD expect rigid reverse-chronological ordering23. |
| **Selecting which items appear** (omitting irrelevant roles to save space) | **Folklore / Prohibited** | uni-assist and TUM explicitly forbid chronological omissions, neutralizing the strategy of curating only "relevant" experiences2. |
| **Rewriting descriptions** (adopting the target programme's vocabulary) | **Folklore** | No admissions rubric rewards echoing programmatic vocabulary in the CV; this is exclusively assessed in the motivation letter1. |
| **Adding skills/claims not otherwise present** (unsubstantiated keywords) | **Folklore / High Risk** | Demands for APEL (Accredited Prior Experiential Learning) require documented evidence. Unsupported claims border on misrepresentation11. |

#### **B4. The Risk Side: Misrepresentation**

Tailoring a CV to the point of misrepresentation, exaggeration, or strategic omission carries severe, binding consequences. While institutions do not publish a philosophical distinction between "emphasis" and "fabrication," their policies on data integrity are absolute. Charles University (MFDA) states: "Any attempts to mislead the admission committee by providing altered or incomplete documents will result in an automatic disqualification from the admission process"15.  
At the University of Oxford, Wadham College policy defines the threshold: "Misrepresentation includes the failure to state material facts in a UCAS application which there is a duty to disclose, or stating false facts"26. The Rhodes Trust at Oxford explicitly warns that applications are monitored for "embellishments and inaccuracies," stating that if they suspect "material misrepresentation of your qualifications, experiences or achievements... your application may be withdrawn, or, where appropriate, an offer of a scholarship may be rescinded"27. Cambridge's Judge Business School maintains an identical stance, confirming that the University "will withdraw your application if any fake documents or false information have been submitted"29. Omission of history to create a tailored narrative directly violates these transparency mandates.

### **Track C: Making an Unfamiliar Institution Legible**

For applicants from regions such as Azerbaijan, Central Asia, Pakistan, and Sub-Saharan Africa, translating the legitimacy of their local institutions to European committees is the single most critical function of the application package.

#### **C1. Official Guidance on Institutional Legibility**

European admissions heavily outsource the interpretation of foreign credentials to centralized evaluation bodies operating under the Lisbon Recognition Convention and the ENIC-NARIC networks11. The burden of proof regarding institutional legitimacy is shifted away from the CV and onto formal credential evaluation and standardization metrics.

* **Credential Evaluation (Binding):** Applications to German institutions routinely require a Vorprüfungsdokumentation (VPD) from uni-assist, which evaluates non-German bachelor's degrees against the Anabin database to establish equivalence and university entrance qualification1. For North American institutions, World Education Services (WES) Course-by-Course evaluations are the standard, converting foreign credit hours and grades to a 4.0 scale methodology32.  
* **Grading Scale Statements (Binding):** When applying directly, applicants are explicitly instructed to provide official grading frameworks. Charles University (MFDA) mandates: "Transcript(s) of records should show grades... It should also provide information about the grading scale sufficient to interpret results"15.  
* **Relative Performance (Advisory):** Charles University formally welcomes "information on the relative performance of an applicant in his or her cohort (i.e. GPA percentile)" to contextualize absolute grades from unfamiliar grading systems15.  
* **ECTS Equivalence (Binding):** CBS explicitly instructs applicants converting from non-ECTS credit systems that they must know "how many credits a full academic year equals in that system" and "the prescribed length of a full bachelor's degree"4.  
* **Diploma Supplement (Advisory/Binding):** The Diploma Supplement, a document standardized across the European Higher Education Area (EHEA), is crucial for explaining the qualification level, learning outcomes, and the national education system34. Where a formal Diploma Supplement is not issued (e.g., in Azerbaijan), uni-assist requires applicants to submit all available curricular regulations if the degree awards fewer than 60 ECTS per year35.

To illustrate the complexity of grade translation that evaluators manage outside the CV, WES utilizes sophisticated iGPA conversions for international credentials:

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **India** | 10-Point CGPA | O / A+ (9.0 \- 10.0) | 4.0 (A)33 |
| **China** | 100-Point Percentage | 优秀 Excellent (90 \- 100\) | 4.0 (A)33 |

#### **C2. Institutional Website URLs**

Including the URL of the applicant's prior institution in the CV's education entry is fully supported by primary sources. As noted in Section A4, Charles University explicitly prefers hypertext links to identify schools and organizations15. Furthermore, the official Europass XML schema (the standard for European academic mobility) explicitly contains a Website data field (europass:WebsiteType) within both the EducationExperienceType and Organisation nodes16. Filling this field is an expected, architecturally supported capability of the schema. This transitions the practice of including institutional URLs from mere coaching folklore to structurally supported standard practice within the European Learning Model (ELM)38.

#### **C3. Institutional Rankings on the CV**

Institutional rankings frequently define eligibility, but adding the ranking *as text on the CV* remains unsupported. The DIGICREA Erasmus Mundus Master program (Université Libre de Bruxelles) dictates that applicants must hold a degree "from an accredited university (listed in the IAU's List of Universities of the World or in some of three top university rankings Shanghai, QS and Times)"40. If the university is unlisted, the applicant must separately email "proof of quality"40. However, no primary source instructs the applicant to explicitly write "Ranked \#1 in Azerbaijan" on the CV itself. While establishing the institution's accreditation is critical, attempting to do so via CV vanity metrics is coaching folklore. Admissions committees and ENIC-NARIC credential evaluators verify accreditation through internal databases (like Anabin) and state-authorized lists31.

#### **C4. Coursework on the CV**

Adding lists of coursework or modules to a CV displaces valuable space and fundamentally duplicates required application forms. Official guidance actively diverts coursework lists away from the CV into separate, standardized documents. TUM requires a "Curriculum of your Bachelor program" containing "the contents and the learning outcomes of your courses" alongside official Transcripts of Records2. Charles University (MFDA) asks applicants to provide "syllabi of courses studied at the Bachelor's level" in a separate, compiled document15. The European Learning Model uses specific Application Profiles (like LOQ \- Learning Opportunities and Qualifications) to map these outcomes systematically42. Suggesting that a master's applicant list relevant modules on their CV to "tailor" their profile is coaching folklore; the institutional mechanism for this evaluation exists entirely outside the CV.

### **Track D: Competency Frameworks and Skill Sections**

#### **D1. Generic Competencies**

No published evidence was found that isolated lists of generic competencies (e.g., "critical thinking," "analytical thinking," "teamwork") on a CV influence master's admissions outcomes. The US-based Council of Graduate Schools (CGS) conducted a comprehensive study on holistic graduate admissions, identifying critical and analytical thinking as highly valued attributes for master's degree success18. However, the report explicitly notes the mechanism for assessment: "Letters of recommendation are used in weighing a wide range of cognitive and non-cognitive attributes"18. Cognitive potential and soft skills are evaluated holistically via reference letters, personal statements, and interviews6, rendering unevidenced lists of soft skills on a CV completely inert.

#### **D2. Evidenced vs. Declared Skills**

While institutions do not publish a specific policy penalizing "unevidenced" skills on a CV, the demand for verifiable documentation governs the entire European admissions architecture. The ENIC-NARIC manual emphasizes the rigorous assessment of non-traditional learning and Accredited Prior Experiential Learning (APEL), requiring structural evidence for recognition11. Simply declaring a skill on a CV without contextual evidence (a project, publication, or professional role) bypasses the academic burden of proof.

#### **D3. Self-Rated Proficiency**

Self-rated technical proficiency levels (e.g., "Advanced Python," "Novice C++") lack standardization and primary-source support in master's admissions. Conversely, language proficiency is strictly regulated and standardized using the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR), with explicit levels (e.g., B2, C1) required and validated by recognized test scores (IELTS, TOEFL)12.

#### **D4. Programme-Specific Vocabulary**

Advising an applicant to rewrite their past technical work using the specific vocabulary of the target programme (e.g., retroactively rebranding basic data analysis as "AI-focused") lacks primary-source support. As established in B4, altering historical documents or misrepresenting past academic realities borders on admissions fraud and risks disqualification15.

### **Evidence-Quality Verdict**

The findings in this report are grounded in the following hierarchies of evidence:

* **Track A (Admissions reading behavior):** Conclusions rest on **published policy** and **on-the-record admissions practice** (formal rubrics from TUM, CBS, and Oxford). *Weakest link:* The complete absence of empirical data regarding the exact time spent per file by European committees.  
* **Track B (Tailoring outcomes):** Conclusions rest exclusively on **coaching folklore**. The premise that CV tailoring enhances master's admissions outcomes is entirely unevidenced. Institutional policies directly contradict the practice by requiring gapless, complete histories. *Weakest link:* The absolute null result in empirical literature regarding CV manipulation in academic admissions.  
* **Track C (Institutional legibility):** Conclusions rest on **published policy** (uni-assist directives, ENIC-NARIC guidelines, ELM documentation, and university application handbooks). *Weakest link:* The inclusion of university rankings directly on the CV relies on coaching folklore, despite university eligibility relying heavily on published ranking indexes and accreditation databases.  
* **Track D (Skills sections):** Conclusions rest on **empirical study** (CGS analysis of graduate attributes) and **coaching folklore**. *Weakest link:* The assumption that admissions readers penalize generic skill lists; the evidence merely indicates they assess these traits through other documents, making the CV list inert rather than actively penalized.
