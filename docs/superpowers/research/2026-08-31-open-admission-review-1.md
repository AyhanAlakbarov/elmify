# Verifying the Safety and Recognition of Foreign Higher-Education Institutions for Self-Funded International Master's Students
### Launch markets: Azerbaijan and Türkiye | Prepared 31 August 2026

## TL;DR
- **The screen works, but it is layered, not a single lookup.** An institution is only "safe" for a self-funded master's student when it clears four independent gates: (1) it is accredited/quality-assured in its home system (EQAR/DEQAR in Europe; national registers elsewhere), (2) it actually awards its own degree (or its validation chain is documented), (3) the *student's* home country will recognise the qualification, and (4) — in visa-controlled destinations — the institution holds stable sponsor standing. Accreditation answers none of the other three; each is a separate question with a separate answer.
- **For the two launch markets, home-country recognition is the binding constraint and it is NOT a list.** Türkiye (YÖK) operates a searchable recognition query (e-Devlet) plus a published *blacklist* of non-recognised institutions and a ranking-based equivalence rule; Azerbaijan (TKTA) is purely case-by-case nostrification with no published list. Neither publishes a positive master list of recognised foreign institutions, so recognition cannot be pre-computed the way Germany's anabin, China's MOE list, Ireland's IRQ or Poland's system allow.
- **The best affordable, screenable floor is Germany's tuition-free public sector** (zero tuition; €992 net/month or €11,904 for the 2026 blocked-account visa minimum, per the official Make it in Germany portal), whose institutions are verifiable in DEQAR *and* recognised by their own regulator. Türkiye's own public universities are cheap in lira terms and a strong regional option. The most dangerous failure mode is a properly "accredited" provider that is nonetheless unrecognised in the student's home country or excluded from a post-study-work route — a safe-looking floor that is not.

## PART A — THE SCREEN

### A general principle: five questions, five answers
Accreditation, degree-awarding power, home-country recognition, visa-sponsor standing, and enforcement history are **five distinct facts**. A provider can be accredited yet award no degrees of its own; can be a real university yet unrecognised in a given home country; can be recognised yet lose its visa-sponsor licence mid-degree. The screen must therefore be run as five checks, never collapsed into one.

### A1. Accreditation and quality assurance
**EHEA / Europe — EQAR and DEQAR.** The European Quality Assurance Register (EQAR) registers quality-assurance agencies that comply with the European Standards and Guidelines (ESG); its database, DEQAR, publishes the external QA reports those agencies produce. By the end of 2024 DEQAR held 109,117 reports, with 48 of 57 EQAR-registered agencies uploading. It is machine-readable (CSV, API, and admin interface; base institution list synchronised daily with OrgReg/ETER).

*What it does not tell you.* DEQAR was **not built to catalogue programmes** — EQAR states plainly it "was not built to search by programmes." Participation by agencies is **voluntary**, so absence from DEQAR is not proof an institution is unaccredited; a legitimate institution may have been reviewed by a credible-but-unregistered agency. Coverage is European. Critically, a QA report at *institutional* level says nothing about whether a *specific master's* is accredited — in several systems programmes require separate accreditation. So DEQAR answers "was this institution/programme externally reviewed by an ESG-compliant agency?" — not "is this degree recognised anywhere."

**Machine-readability:** high (API/CSV). **Terms:** free, public.

**Elsewhere:** national accreditation registers (e.g., Georgia's NCEQE authorisation, Poland's system, Ireland's IRQ) perform the equivalent function domestically. The key transferable rule: *always confirm whether the country accredits at institution level, programme level, or both.* EQAR itself warns "in some countries not only the HEIs need to have formal accreditation/recognition status but also the study programmes."

### A2. Degree-awarding powers versus teaching rights
This distinction is real and a common source of student confusion. The clearest published model is the **UK**: "Recognised Bodies" have degree-awarding powers; "Listed Bodies" only *deliver* courses leading to a degree awarded by a Recognised Body (validation/franchising). The degree-awarding body remains "ultimately responsible for the academic standards of any awards granted in its name." A student at a Listed Body receives a degree from the validating Recognised Body, not from the teaching college — a genuine difference in what the qualification is.

*How it is published.* In England, degree-awarding powers are verifiable on the **Office for Students Register**; recognised bodies are also set out in Education (Recognised Bodies) Orders on legislation.gov.uk. The UK government "no longer publishes Listed Bodies" pending an OfS data-collection exercise, so the teaching-only tier is currently *less* transparent than the degree-awarding tier — a gap to flag. gov.uk's "check if your university or college can award a degree" tool covers all four UK nations.

*What it does not tell you.* Confirming a Recognised Body has degree-awarding powers does not confirm the *specific college* you enrol at is a legitimate delivery partner, nor that the resulting degree is recognised in your home country. Validation chains must be documented end-to-end.

### A3. Home-country recognition — derived from the ENIC-NARIC directory
Every Lisbon Recognition Convention signatory operates an ENIC-NARIC centre (56 countries). Each maintains a country page with a "Recognised Higher Education Institutions" section. But **most centres do not publish a positive list of recognised *foreign* institutions** — they either point to the *home* country's list of its own recognised institutions, or assess foreign qualifications **case by case**. ENIC-NARIC's own guidance is that "the most straightforward way to check the status of an institution is to refer to the official lists of recognised/accredited institutions in the country where the institution is based" — i.e., the check is normally done against the *awarding* country's register, then a case-by-case comparability assessment is made by the student's home centre.

**The list-publishing bodies (the computable gates).** A body that publishes an actual list of *recognised foreign institutions* turns recognition from a disclosure into a computable gate. Confirmed list-publishers:
- **Germany — anabin** (run by ZAB under the KMK). Free, public, and the binding domestic reference. It rates foreign *institutions* H+ (equivalent to a German Hochschule), H− (not equivalent), or H+/− (case-by-case by programme), and separately rates the *degree*. Recognition requires the combination (e.g., H+ institution *and* "gleichwertig"/"entspricht" degree). anabin explicitly "does not claim to be complete," so absence ≠ non-recognition; ZAB then issues an individual Statement of Comparability. This is the single best machine-usable recognition gate found.
- **China — the MOE Foreign Education Supervision list (jsj.moe.gov.cn).** China's Ministry of Education has published "a list of over 10,000 overseas colleges and universities in 44 countries which are accredited by China," created to protect students from bogus providers (per Global Times reporting China's MOE, "MOE lists accredited foreign education institutions to avoid students being cheated"). Caveat from MOE itself: it is "a reference list for the public" — being on it is not a guarantee of successful CSCSE degree authentication, and absence is not automatic failure.
- **Ireland — QQI / Irish Register of Qualifications (IRQ).** A national database of NFQ-recognised qualifications and the providers/awarding bodies quality-assured by QQI. QQI is also NARIC Ireland for foreign-qualification advice. (This lists *Irish* recognised qualifications — its power is as a positive register of what Ireland itself recognises.)
- **Poland — NAWA.** Publishes the Polish higher-education system's recognised institutions and the legal basis for recognising foreign degrees (by international agreement, else by national legislation).

**Beyond the four:** the directory did not surface additional national centres publishing a *positive list of recognised foreign institutions*; the dominant model outside Germany/China is case-by-case assessment plus reliance on the awarding country's own register. France publishes lists of institutions authorised to award specific national titles (e.g., titre d'ingénieur) in its Journal Officiel — a partial, domestic analogue.

#### The launch markets (binding constraint)

**Azerbaijan — TKTA, case-by-case, NO published list.** Recognition ("nostrification") of bachelor's and master's degrees is run by the **Agency for Quality Assurance in Education (Təhsildə Keyfiyyət Təminatı Agentliyi, TKTA)**; doctoral nostrification sits with the Higher Attestation Commission. Azerbaijan joined the Lisbon Convention in April 1997; the process runs through an e-service portal (**nostrifikasiya.edu.az**, via the centralized TMİS system) where the applicant submits documents and tracks status. Legal basis: Cabinet of Ministers Decision No. 64 of 13.05.2003. **Finding: TKTA publishes no positive master list of recognised foreign institutions; every foreign degree is assessed per application.** For a self-funded student this means recognition cannot be pre-confirmed from a public list before enrolment — it is only resolved after graduation, on application. This is the single most important operational fact for the Azerbaijan launch market.

**Türkiye — YÖK, dual system (query tool + blacklist + ranking rule).** Türkiye's Council of Higher Education (YÖK), through its Department of Recognition and Equivalence Services, runs a two-step regime: **tanıma** (recognition — YÖK confirms the foreign institution and programme are authorised to award degrees and the diploma is valid in the awarding country) and **denklik** (equivalence — which Turkish level/field the qualification equals). A student checks recognition through the **e-Devlet query** (turkiye.gov.tr/yok-okul-tanima-belgesi-sorgulama), which returns both recognised and non-recognised institutions; YÖK also publishes a **list of non-recognised ("tanınmayan") institutions** (e.g., ~41 blacklisted for FETÖ/PDY links). Under the **Yurtdışı Yükseköğretim Diplomaları Tanıma ve Denklik Yönetmeliği** as amended 15 March 2024 (Official Gazette No. 32490) and clarified 28 March 2024: graduates of universities ranked in the **top 400** (in at least three of YÖK's accepted rankings — ARWU, CWTS Leiden, QS, THE) get **direct equivalence** with no exam; those in the **top 1000** (in at least two) apply for equivalence but face **no YKS requirement**; outside the top 1000, recognition is possible but individual assessment and possibly a level-determination exam (SBS/STS) may apply, and regulated fields (medicine, dentistry, pharmacy, law) face stricter success-ranking conditions. **Finding: recognition is verified via query, not a positive downloadable list; equivalence is partly rule-based (rankings) and partly case-by-case.** The ranking rule is effectively a computable gate for well-ranked institutions and a case-by-case gate below.

#### Other priority home countries
- **Georgia — NCEQE.** The NARIC centre; recognises foreign education by **verifying authenticity and establishing comparability** — case-by-case, via an e-portal (eqe.ge). Domestically it *authorises* HEIs (compulsory) — that is a positive list of Georgian institutions, not of foreign ones. No published positive list of recognised foreign institutions.
- **Armenia — ArmENIC.** Recognition decisions made by competent bodies; maintains lists of *recognised Armenian* institutions and "recognised international education providers in Armenia" via government systems. Foreign-qualification recognition is assessment-based, not a foreign master list.
- **Kazakhstan — ENIC-Kazakhstan** (Higher Education Development National Center, Ministry of Science and Higher Education). Provides the public recognition service for foreign qualifications; Lisbon Convention ratified. Case-by-case recognition service; no positive foreign master list surfaced.
- **India — UGC.** Under the **UGC (Recognition and Grant of Equivalence to Qualifications obtained from Foreign Educational Institutions) Regulations, 2025** (replacing AIU's role; AIU had historically issued equivalence), equivalence is granted via a **dedicated online portal** (equivalence.ugc.ac.in) — case-by-case, decided by a standing committee (target ~10 working days). **Franchise-arrangement degrees are excluded**; off-shore campus degrees can qualify subject to conditions. Professional fields (medicine, pharmacy, nursing, law, architecture) are outside these regulations (governed by statutory councils). Online/distance degrees were restricted under the 2023 draft.
- **Pakistan — HEC.** HEC does **not attest foreign degrees**; instead it issues **equivalence letters** for foreign qualifications (eservices.hec.gov.pk) and attests those. Case-by-case equivalence; MS/MPhil-equivalent programmes launched after 7 Nov 2013 require an HEC NOC. New Degree Attestation System launched 11 May 2026 (e-attestation).
- **Vietnam — VN-NARIC** (est. 2016, under MOET's quality-management department). Online foreign-degree recognition service, case-by-case, ~20 working days, fee ~$15/credential.
- **Indonesia — Kemdikbud/Ditjen Dikti penyetaraan ijazah.** Case-by-case diploma-equivalence system (not fully re-verified this run — flagged in Part D).

### A4. Visa-sponsor standing
This is a **distinct risk from degree-mill risk**: a properly accredited, recognised institution can still strand students if its sponsor licence is unstable.
- **UK — Register of Licensed Student Sponsors (Home Office).** Published and updated weekly. Statuses matter: a sponsor can be given an **action plan**, a **zero-CAS allocation** (remains on the register but cannot issue new Confirmations of Acceptance for Studies), be **removed pending investigation**, or have its licence **revoked** (strongest sanction; normally a 2-year ban; existing students may be taught out for a limited period). **Live example:** the Home Office **revoked the Bloomsbury Institute's student-sponsor licence on 5 August 2026** (following suspension on 9 June 2026) for failing the Basic Compliance Assessment. Per HESA data via ICEF Monitor, "the Bloomsbury Institute enrolled a total of 1,035 students [in 2024/25]. More than 800 of those, or nearly 80%, were international students with the lion's share (93%) coming from Pakistan"; the BCA thresholds it failed were "a visa refusal rate of less than 10%, an enrolment rate of at least 90%, and a course completion rate of at least 85%" (GOV.UK). History of removals is retrievable (gov.uk publishes the register; third parties mirror revocation history).
- **Other destinations with equivalent registers:** Australia — **CRICOS/PRISMS**; Canada — **Designated Learning Institution (DLI) list**; Ireland — **ILEP** (Interim List of Eligible Programmes) plus new TrustEd Ireland statutory mark; New Zealand — **NZQA**. (Detail per-country not fully re-verified this run — see Part D.)

*What it does not tell you.* Sponsor status is about immigration compliance, not academic quality; a green-rated sponsor can still be academically weak, and a suspended sponsor can be academically excellent but administratively non-compliant.

### A5. Regulatory action and warning lists
- **Oregon Office of Degree Authorization (ODA)** — the most-cited US list of unaccredited degree suppliers and non-approved "accreditors"; recommended even by the FTC. Not comprehensive ("new phony credentialing sources arise all the time").
- **US Department of Education / CHEA** — the authoritative lists of *recognised* accreditors; anything outside them cited by a provider is a red flag. CHEA and several US states (Texas THECB "substandard/fraudulent institutions," Michigan, Maine, Hawaii) publish warning lists.
- **YÖK's non-recognised list** (Türkiye) and **ENIC-NARIC's "Non-recognised HEIs and Diploma Mills"** section are the region-relevant enforcement references.
- **Consumer/aggregator lists** (GetEducated's ~300 suspected mills + ~50 fake accreditors) are useful secondary cross-checks but are not regulators.

## PART C — WHAT WE MUST NEVER SURFACE

### Exclusion criteria (with published examples)
1. **Unaccredited providers presenting as universities / degree mills.** Example: "Harrington University" (aka University of San Moritz / Palmers Green / Devonshire), a mail-drop operation that sold ~70,000 diplomas. **Axact Ltd (Karachi)** is the largest known case: per the New York Times investigation (Declan Walsh, "Fake Diplomas, Real Cash: Pakistani Company Axact Reaps Millions," 17 May 2015), the NYT "analyzed more than 370 websites" bearing Axact's fingerprints — including 145 fictitious universities, 41 high schools, 18 fake accreditation boards and 121 degree portals; CEO Shoaib Ahmed Shaikh was later jailed in what Gulf News (2018) called a "$140m fake diploma scam." "Concordia College and University" (Liberia et al.) is listed by Oregon ODA as a degree mill. Tell: appears on Oregon ODA / Texas THECB / CHEA warning lists, or nowhere in any national register.
2. **Accreditation-mill "accreditors."** Bodies that exist to certify anyone, with no governmental standing. Tell: the "accreditor" is not on the **US Department of Education / CHEA** recognised list (or the relevant national equivalent) — CHEA and ODA both maintain lists of fake accreditors.
3. **Institutions under active regulatory action.** Example: **Bloomsbury Institute** — UK student-sponsor licence **revoked 5 August 2026**. Tell: appears with an action-plan flag, zero-CAS, suspension, or removal on the Home Office register (or CRICOS/DLI equivalents).
4. **Providers whose qualifications are unrecognised in our students' home countries.** Example: any institution on **YÖK's non-recognised list** (~41 blacklisted); any provider whose degree TKTA/HEC/UGC declines to nostrify/equivalence. Tell: fails the YÖK e-Devlet query, or is H− in anabin.
5. **Provider categories systematically excluded from post-study-work routes.** In the UK, the **Graduate Route requires the sponsor to be "a higher education provider with a track record of compliance"** (Immigration Rules Appendix Graduate, rule GR 4.1; gov.uk "Student Sponsor – Track Record" status). Students at providers *without* track-record status — and, as a consequence, those studying only under a validation arrangement where the teaching body is not itself the track-record HE sponsor — **cannot access the Graduate Route** even if the degree itself is genuine and recognised. This is the "safe-looking floor that is not."

### Name the tells (reusable heuristic)
Observable from a provider's *own* materials, each predicting screen failure:
- **Guaranteed-admission / "no exams, no coursework" language**, or admission "on payment."
- **An accreditor with no governmental standing** — check the named accreditor against USDE/CHEA or the national register; a self-invented or "international" accreditor is a red flag.
- **Recruitment-agent commission structures foregrounded** / aggressive agent-driven marketing with guaranteed offers.
- **A campus address that is a serviced office or mail-drop** (Harrington used UK mail-drops; cross-check the address).
- **The provider asserts its own accreditation but it appears in no regulator register** — the sourcing rule: never trust an institution's self-assertion; verify against the regulator.
- **Confusion of "recognition" with "accreditation"** in marketing (e.g., claiming a QS/THE ranking implies home-country equivalence — it does not).
- **Names mimicking real universities** (e.g., "Dublin Metropolitan University," which ODA notes has no authority to issue Irish degrees).

## PART D — WHERE THE SCREEN CANNOT REACH

Stated plainly: for the following, Part A's mechanisms do **not** produce an automatable clear, so **a consultant check is mandatory, not optional**, and must not be papered over with a weaker proxy.

1. **Azerbaijan as a *home*-recognition gate.** Because TKTA publishes no positive list and nostrifies case-by-case *after* graduation, no foreign institution can be pre-cleared for an Azerbaijani student from public sources. Every recommendation to an Azerbaijani student carries irreducible recognition uncertainty until nostrification is applied for.
2. **Türkiye below the top-1000 ranking band.** For institutions outside the rankings, YÖK equivalence is individually assessed (possible SBS/STS exam), so outcomes cannot be pre-computed; regulated professions add further conditionality.
3. **Case-by-case-only home countries generally** — Georgia (NCEQE), Armenia (ArmENIC), Kazakhstan (ENIC-KZ), India (UGC portal), Pakistan (HEC equivalence), Vietnam (VN-NARIC), Indonesia (Ditjen Dikti): none publishes a positive list of recognised *foreign* institutions, so recognition is a post-hoc assessment, not a gate you can pre-clear.
4. **Countries outside the Lisbon Convention / ENIC-NARIC network** and destinations without a public accreditation register or sponsor register — no mechanism yields an answer; manual verification required.
5. **UK "Listed Bodies" (teaching-only providers)** — currently not published pending the OfS data-collection exercise, so the validation tier is not automatically verifiable right now.
6. **Indonesia's penyetaraan ijazah, and the ILEP/CRICOS/DLI/NZQA sponsor registers** — not re-verified to primary-source depth this run; treat as "consultant check" pending confirmation.

## RECOMMENDATIONS (staged, with thresholds that would change them)

**Stage 1 — Build the screen before the catalogue (immediate).** Encode Part A as a five-gate checklist and require *all five* to pass before any institution is surfaced. Wire in the two machine-readable positives first: **anabin** (Germany) and the **MOE list** (China), plus **DEQAR** for European QA and the **UK Home Office register** (weekly) for sponsor standing. *Threshold to expand automation:* if any of Azerbaijan/Georgia/Armenia begins publishing a positive foreign-institution list, promote that market from "consultant check" to "hard gate."

**Stage 2 — Anchor the affordable floor on Germany's public sector (immediate).** It is the only large sector that is simultaneously low-cost (€0 tuition, ~€300 semester fee, €11,904 blocked account), open/rule-based in admissions, DEQAR-verifiable, and domestically recognised (H+). Make it the default recommendation for cost-sensitive students, with **İTÜ / Turkish public universities** as the low-lira regional alternative. *Threshold:* re-check Norway after 1 August 2026 — if public universities set fees to zero and living-cost proof (NOK 170,368/yr for 2026-27 per the Study in Norway portal) is manageable, add it; if fees stay full, keep it excluded.

**Stage 3 — Gate every recommendation on the student's home-country recognition mechanism (immediate).** For **Türkiye**, run the YÖK e-Devlet query and apply the top-400/top-1000 ranking rule at the *year of enrolment*. For **Azerbaijan and all other case-by-case markets**, state explicitly in-product that recognition is resolved only on post-graduation application and cannot be guaranteed — never imply a list-clear where none exists.

**Stage 4 — Screen post-study-work separately (before promoting any UK/Australia/Canada option).** Confirm "track record of compliance" sponsor status for UK, and that the student's provider — not just a validating partner — is the licensed sponsor. *Threshold:* drop any provider that loses track-record status or is placed on an action plan.

**Stage 5 — Hard-exclude on the Part C tells** and cross-check every candidate against ODA/CHEA/YÖK-blacklist/anabin-H− before it can ever appear.

## WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR PLANS (explicit flags)

- **A machine-readable register combining accreditation *and* recognition** would be transformative. The closest today is **Germany's anabin** (institution H-status *plus* degree equivalence, free, daily-syncable) — it is effectively an accreditation+recognition gate for the German market and the model to build on. **DEQAR** is machine-readable for QA but does **not** carry recognition. No single register combines both across markets.
- **If Azerbaijan, Georgia or Armenia published a positive list of recognised foreign institutions, it would convert recognition from a post-hoc disclosure into a hard gate for our primary population.** As of 31 August 2026, **none does** — all three are case-by-case. This is the single highest-value change to watch; a TKTA or NCEQE positive list would materially de-risk the launch markets.
- **A public, low-cost, genuinely low-selectivity sector that clears the screen = the best floor this product can offer.** **Germany's tuition-free public universities** are exactly this: €0 tuition, ~€300 semester fee, €11,904 blocked-account visa minimum (2026, Make it in Germany), open/rule-based admission at many programmes, DEQAR-verifiable, domestically recognised. **Türkiye's public universities** are the regional low-lira analogue. **Norway** is a moving target: since 2023 non-EEA students pay full tuition, but from **1 August 2026** universities may set their own fees (including zero) subject to parliamentary approval — worth re-checking, though Norway's living-cost minimum (NOK 170,368/yr for 2026-27, per the official Study in Norway portal) remains a high floor regardless.
- **A provider category that passes accreditation but is excluded from post-study work** is the trap. In the UK, **accredited providers without "track record of compliance" sponsor status — and validation-only teaching arrangements — are excluded from the Graduate Route** even when the degree is genuine. This looks like a safe floor and is not; it must be screened explicitly wherever a post-study-work route is part of the value proposition.

## CAVEATS
- **Dates and staleness.** All statuses are as checked on/around 31 August 2026. Accreditation, sponsor licences, tuition, visa minimums and recognition rules change; a stale clear is worse than no answer. Re-date every status before relying on it — especially Türkiye's YÖK denklik rules (last major change March 2024, and politically volatile) and UK sponsor statuses (weekly).
- **Unpublished facts flagged, not estimated.** Acceptance rates are not published for the listed institutions and were not estimated. RWTH's exact numeric English/GPA cutoffs are not stated verbatim on the official programme page (it defers to a central language page); third-party figures (e.g., IELTS 6.0–6.5) are unverified against primary source. - **Recognition ≠ accreditation.** These are treated as separate questions throughout. Being accredited/DEQAR-listed does not mean a degree is recognised in Azerbaijan, Türkiye, or any other home market — that is a separate, often case-by-case, determination.
