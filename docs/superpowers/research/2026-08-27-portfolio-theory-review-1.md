# Optimal Application-Portfolio Choice: A Literature Review

## TL;DR
- The canonical result is Chade & Smith (Econometrica 2006): when application outcomes are **independent**, application costs are **identical across options**, and only the **best realized offer** is kept, a greedy "marginal improvement algorithm" (MIA) provably returns the optimal portfolio — the user's understanding is correct, but only under those exact conditions.
- Greedy optimality **breaks** the moment the user's real features appear: with **heterogeneous per-option costs** the problem becomes NP-complete (a knapsack) and greedy loses any guarantee (Kapur & Hong 2022); with **correlated outcomes** the optimal portfolio is no longer nested and a non-greedy recursive algorithm is required (Ali & Shorrer, AER 2025).
- **No robust / ordinal-probability version of this specific problem exists** — every result assumes known, calibrated cardinal admission probabilities and cardinal utilities. This is a genuine, publishable gap; the closest adjacent tools are the matching-market "optimal truncation" results.

## Key Findings
1. **Chade & Smith (2006) is proven, not conjectured**, but narrowly: independence, identical costs, single kept prize. The optimal set is "more aggressive than the best singletons, less aggressive than the sequentially optimal set," is upward-diversified, and **contains no safety school** under independence.
2. **Hard budget / heterogeneous costs**: cardinality-constrained (equal cost) case stays exactly solvable and nested; the knapsack (unequal cost) case is NP-complete, but admits an FPTAS. The generic monotone-submodular guarantees — (1−1/e) for cardinality (Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher 1978) and for knapsack (Sviridenko) — apply but are superseded by exact/FPTAS results tailored to this objective.
3. **Correlated outcomes** are the single most important departure for the user: correlation is exactly what rationalizes safety schools, and it kills the greedy/nesting structure. Ali & Shorrer (2025) is the state of the art.
4. **Two-stage (admission-then-funding) nested lotteries** are covered by Chade & Smith's general per-option prize-distribution formulation *only if options stay independent*; a shared funder re-introduces correlation and breaks greedy.
5. **Precedence / offer-in-hand constraints** are essentially unaddressed in this literature.
6. **Utilities are assumed cardinal primitives** everywhere; probabilities are assumed known and calibrated per-student. Both the ordinal-utility and robust-probability variants are open.
7. **No credible empirical support exists for any specific reach/target/safety ratio** (e.g., "2/4/2"). The applied education literature documents *behavior* and *responsiveness to cost*, not the optimality of a mix.

## Details

### 1. Chade & Smith, "Simultaneous Search" (Econometrica 2006)
**Full citation.** Hector Chade & Lones Smith, "Simultaneous Search," *Econometrica* 74(5) (September 2006): 1293–1307. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2006.00705.x. Author's published-version PDF: https://www.lonessmith.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/simulsearch-ecma-version.pdf. Working-paper (June 2004) PDF: http://fmwww.bc.edu/repec/esNASM04/up.2651.1071707397.pdf. Cowles Foundation DP 1556: https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cowles-discussion-paper-series/1844/.

**What it formalizes.** A decision maker chooses, once and for all, a subset S of N ranked stochastic options at cost c per option. Each option i "succeeds" (e.g., admits) independently with probability α_i and yields cardinal utility u_i; only the best realized success is exercised. Options are ordered u_1 > u_2 > … > u_N, typically with the inverse ordering α_1 < α_2 < … < α_N (better options are harder to get). Gross value of a set is f(S) = Σ z_(i) ρ_(i−1)(S), where z_i = α_i u_i and ρ is the probability of rejection by all better schools already in the set; net value is v(S) = f(S) − c|S|. This "downward-recursive" structure gives the key identity f(U + L) = f(U) + ρ(U) f(L) for any upper set U above lower set L.

**What it proves.**
- **Lemma 2 (Value Submodularity):** v is a submodular set function; in the general version each option i carries an arbitrary prize c.d.f. G_i and v(S) = ∫[1 − Π_{i∈S} G_i(u)] du − c|S|. Submodular maximization is equivalent to maximizing a convex function over the hypercube and is NP-hard in general.
- **Theorem 1 (MIA optimality):** Despite NP-hardness in general, the MIA finds the exact optimum for this problem in O(N²) steps. MIA: pick the best singleton i_1 = argmax f({i}); at each step add the option with the largest marginal gain f(S+i) − f(S); stop (net-value problem) when the marginal gain falls below the incremental cost c(n) − c(n−1). The published stopping rule allows a general **increasing cost schedule c(n)** in the *number* of applications, but costs remain **identical across options** — heterogeneous per-option costs are NOT covered.
- **Structural theorems.** (a) Optimal portfolios are **nested**: the optimal (k+1)-set contains the optimal k-set (this nesting is precisely what makes greedy work). (b) The optimal set is **"more aggressive" (riskier) than the best singletons but less aggressive than the sequentially optimal (Weitzman 1979) set.** (c) **Upward diversification:** with low enough costs the optimal set eventually adds a distinctly riskier option, and connected "intervals" of similar-risk options are generally not optimal (the optimal set "contains gaps"). (d) **No safety school under independence:** "unless the options have correlated success rates, this conventional wisdom [include a safety] is false… if a tier-two school is your best solo possibility, your optimal portfolio cannot include tier-three schools."

**Exactly which conditions the greedy result needs.**
- **Independence** of successes across options (the paper explicitly rationalizes this by conditionally-iid signals with a fully known type).
- **Identical costs** across options (a common c, or a schedule c(n) in the count).
- **Single prize per option / keep-the-best** ("maximax") structure.
- The ordering results additionally use the ranking of expected payoffs z_i and, for some comparative statics, first-order stochastic-dominance shifts — these are **cardinal**, not ordinal, conditions.

**Limitations / counterexamples stated in the paper itself.**
- **Correlation.** Section on "Correlated Values" (working paper §5.1): with positively correlated successes, "getting into any school weakly raises the admission chance to any other school." They show (i) a small amount of correlation does not overturn a *strictly* optimal set — "the MIA is defined by inequalities, and so by continuity, a small amount of correlation does not affect the optimality of a strictly optimal set — barring ties"; and (ii) safety schools can *only* be rationalized once successes are correlated. They give an example with imperfect correlation where MIA still works but do **not** claim MIA is optimal for general correlation.
- **Heterogeneous costs.** The paper's tractability rests on identical costs; it does not treat option-specific application fees.

### 2. Extensions along the user's four real-problem axes

**(A) Hard budget / cardinality / knapsack constraints and submodular guarantees.**
- **Max Kapur & Sung-Pil Hong, "The College Application Problem," arXiv:2205.01869 (v2, May 2022).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01869. This is the paper that most exactly matches the user's optimization: maximize v(X) = E[max{t_0, max_{j∈X} t_j Z_j}] subject to Σ_{j∈X} g_j ≤ H, with independent Bernoulli Z_j (admit prob f_j), cardinal utilities t_j, outside option t_0, per-option fee g_j, budget H. Results, verified against the PDF: (i) the objective is nondecreasing submodular; (ii) **equal fees (cardinality budget h):** optimal portfolios are **nested in the budget**, giving an exact O(hm) algorithm — greedy is exact; (iii) **unequal fees (knapsack):** the decision problem is **NP-complete** (reduction from binary knapsack), nesting fails, and "the greedy algorithm [does not] offer any performance guarantee"; (iv) they nonetheless give a branch-and-bound routine, an exact pseudo-polynomial DP (O(Hm + m log m)), and a **fully polynomial-time approximation scheme (FPTAS)** producing a (1−ε)-optimal solution in O(m³/ε), plus a simulated-annealing heuristic. They explicitly note that Fisher–Nemhauser–Wolsey (1978) gives only an asymptotic (1−1/e) bound for the equal-cost case, "whereas we show that the same algorithm is exact," and that knapsack-submodular relaxation methods (Badanidiyuru–Vondrák 2014; Kulik et al. 2013) also give (1−1/e) but are superseded by their FPTAS. (Kapur's `OptimalApplication.jl` solver implements these algorithms.)
- **Generic submodular guarantees (background).** For monotone submodular maximization under a cardinality constraint, the greedy algorithm is (1−1/e)-optimal — George Nemhauser, Laurence Wolsey & Marshall Fisher, "An analysis of approximations for maximizing submodular set functions—I," *Mathematical Programming* 14 (1978): 265–294. Under a single knapsack constraint the same (1−1/e) is achievable (Sviridenko 2004; Badanidiyuru–Vondrák 2014). These bound the *worst case* for the general submodular class; the college objective is an "easy" instance where exact/FPTAS results are available.
- **Olszewski & Vohra, discrete generalization of simultaneous search,** *Discrete Applied Mathematics* 200 (19 Feb 2016). Author record: https://www.sciencedirect.com/author/7005761644/rakesh-v-vohra. They generalize Chade–Smith to maximizing a weighted sum of utilities minus costs subject to a submodular capacity constraint, and prove that when the submodular function **depends only on the number of objects**, the greedy algorithm finds the optimum; they also show the general case has **no simple (greedy) solution**, complementing Chade–Smith rather than subsuming it.
- **CS/OR "ProbeMax" and stochastic probing.** The non-adaptive ProbeMax problem — max_{|S|≤k} E[max_{i∈S} X_i] with independent X_i — is exactly the cardinality-constrained independent version of the user's objective. Because it is monotone submodular, greedy gives (1−1/e) (via NWF78); a PTAS exists (Chen, Hassani, Karbasi et al., NIPS 2016), improved to an EPTAS by Segev & Singla, "Efficient Approximation Schemes for Stochastic Probing and Selection-Stopping," arXiv:2007.13121, and Fu, Li, Xu et al., "A PTAS for a Class of Stochastic Dynamic Programs," ICALP 2018 (arXiv:1805.07742). **Adaptive** ProbeMax (probe, observe, then decide next probe) is the sequential-search analogue; Munagala's LP rounding gives a constant factor and Gupta & Nagarajan (IPCO 2013) bound the **adaptivity gap at 3**, i.e., simultaneous vs. sequential differ by at most a factor of 3. **How it maps:** ProbeMax = the user's problem with a *pure cardinality budget, independent outcomes, and "probe = apply"*; it does NOT capture heterogeneous costs (that is the submodular-knapsack probing variant, Adamczyk–Sviridenko–Ward, "Submodular Stochastic Probing on Matroids," arXiv:1310.4415, giving (1−1/e)/(k_in+k_out+1)) or correlation.

**(B) Correlated outcomes — the decisive axis.**
- **S. Nageeb Ali & Ran I. Shorrer, "Hedging When Applying: Simultaneous Search with Correlation," *American Economic Review* 115(2) (February 2025): 571–598.** DOI: 10.1257/aer.20211137. PDF: https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.psu.edu/dist/2/100983/files/2025/01/AliShorrer.pdf. Model: applicant picks portfolio P before learning a single **common score** S (atomless c.d.f. F); school i admits iff S ≥ τ_i; correlation arises because rejection by one school is bad news about S and hence about all more-selective schools. **What it proves:** (Prop. 1) more optimistic beliefs (likelihood-ratio sense) ⇒ more aggressive portfolio; (Prop. 2) more risk-loving (Arrow–Pratt) ⇒ more aggressive; **(Theorem 1)** whenever the applicant applies to more than one school, the optimal portfolio includes **both reaches and safeties**, and the optimal (k+1)-portfolio "spreads out" relative to the optimal k-portfolio. **Two mechanisms:** a "bad-news effect" (a safety is the best backup conditional on top-choice rejection) and a "risk-loving effect" (backups insure the downside, pushing the top choice upward). **Crucially for algorithms:** their algorithm is polynomial but **"is not greedy, unlike that proposed by Chade and Smith (2006)… optimal portfolios [in the common-score model] typically are not nested,"** so the greedy/MIA approach fails under correlation. A bridging extension (school-specific scores with independent + common components) shows that **so long as schools put any weight on the common component, every optimal portfolio of ≥2 schools contains at least one safety and one reach.** The paper also delivers the optimal rank-order list for single-tie-breaking matching and handles "tier constraints."
- **Hector Chade, Gregory Lewis & Lones Smith, "Student Portfolios and the College Admissions Problem," *Review of Economic Studies* 81(3) (July 2014): 971–1002.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdu003. PDF: https://www.lonessmith.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/Student-Portfolios-and-the-College-Admissions-Problem.pdf. Embeds the portfolio problem in a two-college decentralized Bayesian **equilibrium** where colleges set admission standards like market-clearing prices. **What it proves/shows:** student-college sorting can fail — "weaker students sometimes apply more aggressively, and the weaker college might impose higher standards"; it applies the framework to affirmative action (minority applicants build portfolios "as if they were majority students of higher caliber"). It **models** correlation and allows it but, being a two-college model, **abstracts from the full multi-option portfolio structure** — it is not a general algorithm paper.
- **Behavioral counterpart — Alex Rees-Jones, Ran Shorrer & Chloe Tergiman, "Correlation Neglect in Student-to-School Matching," *AEJ: Microeconomics* 16(3) (August 2024): 1–42.** DOI: 10.1257/mic.20200407. PDF: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26734/w26734.pdf. Incentivized lab experiments: subjects apply near-optimally under **independent** admissions but, under a **common priority (correlation)**, become "substantially more aggressive and fail to include attractive 'safety' options," even within-subject across payoff-equivalent scenarios — evidence that people find the correlated (i.e., realistic) version genuinely hard.

**(C) Two-stage nested lotteries (admission → funding).**
- Chade & Smith's **general formulation** (each option i characterized by an arbitrary prize c.d.f. G_i) can, under independence, absorb a per-option compound lottery: "admitted with prob a_i, then funded with prob b_i for high utility, else lower utility" is just a three-point G_i. Submodularity (Lemma 2) holds for general G_i. **Caveat:** the MIA optimality (Theorem 1) is proved for the **binary accept/reject-single-prize** structure; greedy optimality for genuinely multi-valued per-option prize distributions is not established at the same level of generality in the paper, and Kapur & Hong likewise assume Bernoulli single-prize options. I found **no paper that explicitly models the admission-then-funding two-stage lottery as such.** The practically important case — a **shared funder** (one scholarship board across many programmes) — is exactly correlation across options and therefore falls under Ali–Shorrer, where greedy fails.

**(D) Precedence / offer-in-hand / fixed-order constraints.**
- This is the **least-developed axis.** Simultaneous search assumes no ordering; sequential search (Martin Weitzman, "Optimal Search for the Best Alternative," *Econometrica* 47(3) (1979): 641–654) solves ordering via a reservation-value index (Pandora's box) but assumes free recall and no precedence. The nearest treatment of "you must hold something to claim another" is **Ying-Ju Chen, "Simultaneous Search, Reservation Fees, and Sequential Outcomes," *Production and Operations Management* 34(11) (2025): 3549–3567** (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10591478251338949), where a searcher pays reservation fees to keep earlier offers "alive" for recall — adjacent to offer-in-hand requirements but not a precedence-constrained portfolio model. **I found no paper solving the E[max] portfolio problem under precedence/offer-in-hand constraints; treat this as an open problem.**

### 3. Safety options and outside options
- **Under independence there is provably no safety school** (Chade & Smith 2006; the optimal set is upward-diversified and "more aggressive than the best singletons"). Students "gamble up," not down.
- **Safety schools are optimal precisely under correlation** (Ali & Shorrer 2025, Theorem 1): the bad-news effect makes a less-selective school the best backup once you condition on top-choice rejection.
- **Outside option / reservation value.** In Chade & Smith the "apply nowhere" value is the reservation baseline (v(∅)=0 after normalization; options with z_i < c are pruned). Ali & Shorrer model an explicit outside option u_o and normalize the payoff of being rejected everywhere to it. Their **risk-loving effect directly validates the user's intuition:** in their worked example, conditioning on getting into a near-certain safety (College 3) "effectively [translates it] into her outside option when choosing between Colleges 1 and 2… the better outside option reduces downside risk… pushing her to reach for College 1." So **a near-certain outside option correctly shifts remaining budget toward reaches** — the marginal value of additional safety collapses once the max is floored, and (under independence) the MIA already prunes any option dominated by u_o, so no separate machinery is needed in the independent case. Under correlation the shift toward reaches is a *proven* comparative static, not merely a heuristic, but it must be computed with the non-greedy recursive algorithm.

### 4. Where do utilities come from?
- **Cardinal utilities are assumed as primitives** throughout: Chade & Smith (u_i), Kapur & Hong (t_j), Ali & Shorrer (u_i, u_o). None elicits them.
- **Structural estimation from application data.** Chao Fu, "Equilibrium Tuition, Applications, Admissions, and Enrollment in the College Market," *Journal of Political Economy* 122(2) (2014): 225–281. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1086/675503. Author PDF linked from https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~cfu/research.html. Fu estimates cardinal preference and ability parameters from NLSY97, explicitly treating application as a "nontrivial portfolio problem," and — because full enumeration is intractable — clusters schools to m=8. This is the leading example of *recovering* the utilities the theory assumes.
- **Ordinal-preference bridge — largely a gap.** The market-design literature works in ordinal rank-order lists (deferred acceptance, Gale–Shapley). Two strands connect ordinal reports to portfolio reasoning: (i) **Agarwal & Somaini's revealed-preference framework** interprets a submitted ROL *as the outcome of an optimal portfolio choice over admission lotteries* (Nikhil Agarwal & Paulo Somaini, "Revealed Preference Analysis of School Choice Models," and their *Econometrica* 2018 demand paper) — but this uses *cardinal beliefs* and is for *estimation*, not prescription; (ii) **Calsamiglia, Fu & Güell, "Structural Estimation of a Model of School Choices," *JPE* 128(2) (2020): 642–680** (DOI: https://doi.org/10.1086/704573) similarly estimate a cardinal model of list submission. The closest *prescriptive* ordinal-input tool is the **optimal-truncation** literature — Peter Coles & Ran Shorrer, "Optimal Truncation in Matching Markets," *Games and Economic Behavior* 87 (2014): 591–615, and Alvin Roth & Uriel Rothblum, "Truncation Strategies in Matching Markets," *Econometrica* 67(1) (1999): 21–43 — which computes how far down a *true ordinal list* to submit given beliefs, effectively an ordinal-preferences-plus-reservation-value problem, but inside stable-matching, not the Chade–Smith E[max] objective. **No paper runs the simultaneous-search optimization on an ordinal ranking plus a stated minimum acceptable outcome; this bridge is open.**

### 5. Knowing admission probabilities — the critical gap
- **All models assume known, exogenous, calibrated per-student probabilities** (α_i, thresholds τ_i, or f_j). Kapur & Hong state this explicitly: independence is "appropriate to assume… as long as f_j are probabilities estimated specifically for this student."
- **Robust / ordinal-probability versions: none found.** A dedicated search (including forward work by Kapur) turned up **no robust-optimization, distributionally-robust, minimax-regret, or interval/ordinal-probability version of the Chade–Smith / college-application E[max] problem.** The large "robust portfolio optimization" literature (minimax-regret Markowitz — e.g., Xidonas et al., "Robust multiobjective portfolio optimization: A minimax regret approach," *European Journal of Operational Research* 262 (2017): 299–305; and distributionally-robust portfolio work) addresses the *concave mean-variance* objective, a structurally different problem, and does not adapt to the convex E[max] objective.
- **Is MIA robust to probability misspecification or to ordinal-only probabilities?** Neither Chade & Smith (2006) nor Ali & Shorrer (2025) proves any such result; both are strictly cardinal. Chade & Smith's own continuity remark ("a small amount of correlation does not affect the optimality of a *strictly* optimal set — barring ties") implies a *local* stability: because the MIA is defined by strict inequalities on expected marginal gains, small perturbations of the α_i do not change a strictly optimal set. But there is **no theorem** that ordinal knowledge of the probability ranking suffices, and the "gaps" and "gamble-up" results demonstrably depend on cardinal magnitudes. **The robustness of the optimal portfolio to uncalibrated probabilities is unproven and is a genuine research gap the user should be aware of.**

### 6. Applied "reach/target/safety" advice — is any ratio empirically supported?
- **No specific ratio (e.g., 2 reach / 4 target / 2 safety) has academic empirical support.** Such ratios are counselling folklore. The credible empirical literature documents application *behavior* and *responses to cost/information*, not the optimality of a mix:
  - **Caroline Hoxby & Christopher Avery, "The Missing 'One-Offs': The Hidden Supply of High-Achieving, Low-Income Students," *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* (Spring 2013): 1–65** (NBER WP 18586, https://www.nber.org/papers/w18586). Documents that high-income high-achievers "generally follow the advice to apply to a few 'par' colleges, a few 'reach' colleges, and a couple of 'safety' schools," while comparable low-income students under-apply and undermatch. This is a *descriptive* contrast, not a validation of the ratio's optimality.
  - **Amanda Pallais, "Small Differences that Matter: Mistakes in Applying to College," *Journal of Labor Economics* 33(2) (2015): 493–520** (NBER WP 19480, https://www.nber.org/papers/w19480). When the ACT raised the number of free score reports from three to four in 1997 (a $6 marginal cost per additional report), Pallais finds **23% of students sent an additional application in response to the cost change**, widened the range of colleges they sent scores to, and low-income ACT-takers attended more-selective colleges. Back-of-the-envelope calculations attribute a substantial earnings gain to the induced upgrading — the associated MIT SEII/press summaries put the improvement at roughly **$10,000 in expected earnings for affected low-income students**. Establishes that students use **simple heuristics** and that tiny costs distort portfolios; says nothing endorsing a fixed mix.
  - **Kehinde Ajayi, "School Choice and Educational Mobility: Lessons from Secondary School Applications in Ghana," *Journal of Human Resources* 59(4) (2024): 1207–…** (https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/59/4/1207; BU PDF: https://www.bu.edu/econ/files/2012/11/Ajayi_EducationalMobility.pdf). Uses Chade & Smith explicitly to analyze constrained choice (students list a limited number — currently six — before knowing exam scores). Finds disadvantaged students apply less aggressively and that differences stem largely from imperfect information; welfare is **concave in the number of choices permitted — expanding the allowed list from 1 to 2 choices more than doubles total welfare and reduces the number of unassigned students by 61%.** **Ajayi & Sidibé, "School Choice Under Imperfect Information"** (SSRN 3524535, Dec 2019 draft) report that **"virtually all students adopt a weakly dominated strategy, and matching outcomes show that approximately 15% of students end up unassigned, while almost half of schools have at least 1 vacancy."** Rees-Jones et al. cite Ajayi's finding that ~92% of students rank schools in an order other than selectivity — i.e., real portfolios routinely violate the theory.
  - **UK UCAS** confirms on its own guidance ("Filling in your UCAS application") that "you can only have five choices in total," with a sub-cap of "a maximum of four courses in any one of medicine, dentistry, veterinary medicine or veterinary science." Centralized systems in Ghana/Turkey/China similarly impose correlated single-exam admission — the settings where Ali & Shorrer's correlated model (safeties optimal) rather than Chade–Smith's independent model applies.
- **Bottom line:** the literature supports *diversification* and shows the *direction* of optimal spread (upward under independence; both directions under correlation), but **no study validates a numeric reach/target/safety ratio.**

### Direct answer: Is a greedy algorithm provably correct, and where does it break?
- **Baseline (independent outcomes, identical costs, single kept prize, known cardinal probabilities and utilities):** **YES — greedy (MIA) is provably optimal** (Chade & Smith 2006, Theorem 1; Kapur & Hong 2022 for the equal-fee cardinality budget, where optimal portfolios are nested and greedy is exact).
- **Hard budget with heterogeneous costs:** **BREAKS.** The problem is NP-complete (Kapur & Hong 2022, reduction from binary knapsack); optimal portfolios are no longer nested and "the greedy algorithm [does not] offer any performance guarantee." Recovery is via FPTAS (1−ε in O(m³/ε)) or pseudo-polynomial DP, not greedy. Generic monotone-submodular knapsack greedy retains only a worst-case (1−1/e) bound (Sviridenko; Badanidiyuru–Vondrák).
- **Correlated outcomes:** **BREAKS.** Optimal portfolios are typically not nested; Ali & Shorrer (2025) require a non-greedy recursive algorithm and prove the optimum contains both reaches and safeties. Chade & Smith note only a *local* result: a strictly optimal set survives *infinitesimal* correlation by continuity.
- **Two-stage nested admission-then-funding lotteries:** **SURVIVES only under independence** (a compound per-option prize distribution is admissible in Chade & Smith's general model, with submodularity intact; but MIA optimality is formally proven only for the binary single-prize case, so multi-valued prizes are not fully covered). With a **shared funder (correlation), it breaks** and reduces to the Ali–Shorrer regime.
- **Precedence / offer-in-hand / fixed-order constraints:** **UNRESOLVED.** No portfolio result exists; the simultaneous-search framework assumes no ordering, and the nearest work (Weitzman 1979 sequential index; Chen 2025 reservation fees) does not deliver a greedy-optimal portfolio under precedence.
- **Ordinal-only utilities:** **BREAKS / UNADDRESSED.** Every result needs cardinal utilities; the "gaps" and "gamble-up" structure depends on cardinal magnitudes. No optimization on an ordinal ranking plus a reservation value exists (the closest is matching-market optimal truncation).
- **Unknown / uncalibrated / ordinal probabilities:** **UNPROVEN — genuine gap.** No robust/DRO/minimax-regret/interval version exists. Only a *local* stability follows from the MIA's strict-inequality definition (small mis-estimates don't flip a strictly optimal set); there is no guarantee under coarse ordinal or interval probability information.

## Recommendations
1. **Diagnose your correlation structure first — it is the decisive fork.** If your programmes/scholarships are judged by *independent* committees on *independent* signals, you are in the Chade–Smith world: run the MIA (best singleton, then largest marginal-gain addition, stop when marginal gain < cost), expect an upward-diversified portfolio with **no pure safety**, and treat a near-certain home-country scholarship as the outside-option floor that licenses reaching higher. If a **shared exam, shared committee, or shared funder** links outcomes (the common case for scholarships and for UCAS/centralized systems), you are in the Ali–Shorrer world: use their non-greedy recursive algorithm, and **deliberately include both a safety and a reach.**
2. **If application fees differ materially, do not trust greedy.** Use a knapsack-aware method: Kapur & Hong's FPTAS or pseudo-polynomial DP (their `OptimalApplication.jl` implements this), or at minimum greedy-by-marginal-gain-per-dollar as a heuristic while knowing it has no guarantee here.
3. **Model admission-then-funding as a single compound prize per option** when funders are distinct and independent — this stays inside the tractable framework. When one funder spans many programmes, model it as a common shock and switch to the correlated algorithm.
4. **Handle unknown probabilities explicitly.** Because no robust theory exists, run the MIA/recursive optimizer across a *range* of plausible probability vectors (a simple scenario/sensitivity sweep) and prefer portfolios that stay optimal or near-optimal across the range; rely on the MIA's local stability only for small mis-estimates. Flag to any stakeholder that this is a heuristic filling a real gap in the literature.
5. **Elicit cardinal utilities, or approximate them.** The theory needs cardinal u_i. If you only have an ordinal ranking, anchor it with a stated minimum acceptable outcome (a reservation value) and a few pairwise trade-offs to impute cardinal values; there is no validated ordinal-only optimizer to fall back on.
6. **Ignore folk ratios.** Do not impose a "2/4/2" mix as if optimal — no evidence supports it. Let the correlation structure and the outside option determine the spread.

**Thresholds that would change these recommendations:** (i) if outcomes are verifiably independent, drop safeties and gamble up; (ii) if any common component exists, add safeties; (iii) if per-option fees vary by more than roughly the marginal-value differences between options, abandon greedy for the FPTAS/DP; (iv) if a near-certain outside option exists whose utility exceeds your would-be safeties, prune those safeties and redirect budget to reaches.

## Caveats
- **Verified vs. abstract-only.** Chade & Smith (2006), Kapur & Hong (2022), and Ali & Shorrer (2025) were verified against full-text PDFs (theorem statements, stopping rule, submodularity proof, and the explicit "not greedy / not nested" statement). Chade–Lewis–Smith (2014), Fu (2014), Hoxby–Avery (2013), Pallais (2015), Ajayi (2024), Olszewski–Vohra (2016), Rees-Jones et al. (2024), and the ProbeMax/stochastic-probing papers were verified against abstracts, author PDFs, and substantial full-text excerpts; specific internal theorem numbers for these were not all inspected line-by-line and are described at the level the sources support. The "$10,000 expected-earnings" figure attributed to Pallais comes from a press/summary characterization of her back-of-the-envelope calculation rather than a verbatim figure in the published paper, and should be cited as such.
- The identity of a working paper titled "Simultaneous Search: Beyond Independent Successes" (associated with Vohra, ACM EC 2019 record) versus the published Olszewski & Vohra (2016, *Discrete Applied Mathematics* 200) could not be fully disambiguated from the sources retrieved; I report the peer-reviewed 2016 DAM result, which is secure, and flag the EC-2019-titled item as likely a related conference version.
- The "no robust/ordinal version exists" and "no precedence-constrained portfolio result exists" claims are **negative results** based on targeted search; very recent (2025–26) working papers may not yet be indexed. A forward-citation sweep of Chade–Smith (2006) and Kapur–Hong (2022) in Google Scholar is advised before treating these gaps as definitive for publication.
- Chade & Smith's general prize-distribution formulation is submodular, but MIA *optimality* is formally established for the binary accept/reject-single-prize structure; do not over-extend the greedy guarantee to arbitrary multi-valued per-option prizes without checking.
