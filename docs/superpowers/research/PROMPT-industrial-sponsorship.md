# Deep research prompt — industrial, corporate and foundation sponsorship

**Status:** ready to run. Written 2026-08-29; de-anchored 2026-09-13 (named funders moved out of the
body into a floor at the end, unsourced figures removed, enumeration made a deliverable). Run twice,
independently, so the results can be adjudicated.

**Why this exists.** `INDUSTRIAL_SPONSORSHIP` is the weakest family in the system. Prior research
found **no authoritative structured directory of corporate or foundation scholarship *schemes***:
360Giving and IRS Form 990 publish grant *history*, not a catalogue of open schemes; Candid is
commercial and closed. Do not spend budget re-checking those three — but if you meet a directory
that does list open schemes, report it; that prior finding is an assumption, not a fact to defend.

This run asks **how a scheme in this family is found at all**, and which schemes our population can
actually use.

---

## Prompt

You are researching **master's-level funding provided by companies, corporate foundations, private
foundations, professional bodies, employers, and political-party or trade-union foundations**, for
students from **Azerbaijan and Türkiye first — these are the launch markets — then Georgia, Armenia,
Central Asia, India, Pakistan, Vietnam, Indonesia and the wider OIC and Turkic world**, studying
anywhere.

### Neutrality rules — read before searching

This prompt deliberately names no scheme in its body. Those rules keep the result a discovery rather
than a confirmation:

1. **No prestige ranking.** A small local company's scheme open to Azerbaijani students is worth more
   here than a famous global one they cannot enter. Do not order, filter or weight results by funder
   size, brand recognition or how often a scheme appears in search results.
2. **Search beyond English.** A scheme funded by a company in Baku or Istanbul is announced in
   Azerbaijani or Turkish, often only there. Search in each origin country's own languages (and
   Russian where it is a working language) and say which languages you actually searched.
3. **No funder type, sector, destination, field or faith is presumed more likely.** Derive where
   funding comes from; do not assume it comes from energy companies, Western foundations, or
   English-speaking destinations.
4. **Report every application route with equal care.** A nominated or employer-only scheme is less
   useful to us, but it is still a finding — do not drop it or shorten its row.
5. **Nothing in this prompt is evidence.** If a fact in your answer matches something written here,
   it must still come from a source you opened. Repeating the prompt back is not verification.

### Part 0 — the enumeration, published before any scheme is researched

Build the list of **candidate funders** first, and publish it as its own section, so coverage is
auditable — what was considered, not only what was found. Derive it along at least these axes:

- **Who has a reason to pay.** Companies that need graduates in our origin countries (local firms,
  and foreign firms operating there); companies that recruit from those countries abroad; foundations
  whose mission names a region, nationality, community, field or faith; professional bodies building
  a pipeline; political-party and trade-union foundations in any country; employers that sponsor
  their own staff.
- **What reaches students on the origin side.** What origin-country universities, career centres,
  student unions, business associations and ministries actually publish as external funding.
- **What reaches students on the destination side.** What universities list as external or partner
  scholarships open to our nationalities.
- **Who is large in each origin economy.** The largest employers, investors and banks per origin
  country, from a public source (stock-exchange listings, investment-agency or statistics-office
  rankings) — not from memory.

For each candidate: the axis that produced it, and later whether a scheme was confirmed (Part A) or
checked and not found (Part C). **Axes you add are worth more than the ones above.**

### Part A — the schemes themselves

One row per scheme.

| Column | Notes |
|---|---|
| Scheme name | In its own language where that differs |
| Funder and type | Company / corporate foundation / private foundation / professional body / employer / party or union foundation / other (describe) |
| **Found via** | Which Part 0 axis and Part B route surfaced it — this is how Part B's routes are measured |
| **Application route** | **`DIRECT` / `NOMINATED` / `EMPLOYER_ONLY` / `HYBRID`.** Quote the sentence |
| Eligible nationalities | Verbatim, including any regional or income-based restriction |
| **Non-financial conditions** | Verbatim: political or confessional alignment, language level, membership, values statements, interviews, community affiliation |
| Eligible fields | State any field restriction |
| Eligible destinations | Where the money can be spent |
| Degree level | Master's specifically |
| What it covers | And whether it is full-cost or partial |
| **Bonding or service obligation** | Whether the student must work for the funder afterwards, for how long, and what happens if they do not. Quote it |
| Annual cycle | With the most recent cycle you can evidence and its date |
| Official URL | The funder's own page |
| Selectivity | Awards, applicants, or a stated rate — **only if published** |

**`Application route` decides usefulness.** A nominated scheme cannot be a safety option, because the
student cannot initiate it. Do not infer the route from tone; quote the sentence.

**Bonding matters nearly as much.** A fully-funded award that binds a graduate to an employer for
several years is a materially different product from an unconditional one.

**Non-financial conditions matter more than money in parts of this family.** A model that expresses
eligibility as a nationality list cannot represent a values statement or a language level, and
foundation-type funders are where such conditions cluster. Record them wherever they appear.

**Aggregator and "list of funders" pages** — including funder lists on other scholarship
programmes' pages — are tip-offs, never findings. Open every lead at its own funder's page. Do not
let one list dominate the enumeration: note which list a lead came from, so a skew toward one
network is visible.

### Part B — how these are found at all

This is the methodological half, and for our purposes the more valuable one.

**Capacity rule.** If the run cannot complete everything, **finish Part 0 and Part B first and let
Parts A, C and D be as long as budget allows.** A reliable method for finding schemes outlasts any
list of schemes, which goes stale within a cycle.

1. **Enumerate the discovery routes that actually work — and derive the enumeration, do not work
   from the list below.** Start from the question: *where does a corporate or foundation scholarship
   leave a public trace?* Every trace is a candidate route — a legal disclosure obligation, a tax
   filing, a marketing channel, a partner's page, a regulator's register, a membership benefit.
   Name who produces each kind of trace in the countries that matter to us, then assess it.

   Routes already known, as a floor rather than a scope: grant-history databases (360Giving,
   Form 990/990-PF, UK Charity Commission, national charity registers), corporate CSR and
   sustainability reports, university partner-scholarship pages, professional-body member benefits,
   embassy and chamber-of-commerce listings. **A route your derivation adds is worth more than any
   of these**, because these are the ones we already knew and none of them solves the problem.

   For each route: coverage, terms, whether machine-readable, **whether it surfaces open schemes or
   only past grants**, and **how many Part A rows it actually produced** in this run.
2. **Assess the "reverse" route.** Is a university's own list of external/partner scholarships a
   more reliable index of corporate schemes than any funder-side source? Answer with evidence, and
   compare universities in origin and destination countries rather than a single prominent one.
3. **Corporate-philanthropy disclosure regimes.** Establish, for every country in scope and every
   common destination, whether large firms must disclose CSR or philanthropic spending, whether
   scholarship schemes are identifiable within those disclosures, and whether any is
   machine-readable. Derive the list of regimes; do not start from a known one.
4. **National and regional business associations** in our origin countries that broker or publish
   scholarships.

### Part C — funders checked and not found

Every Part 0 candidate for whom no published master's scheme could be confirmed, across all origin
countries — not only the launch markets. List them as **checked and not found**, with what was
searched and in which languages, so a later run does not repeat the work.

### Part D — diaspora, community and religious funds

Often the only source for a specific nationality, and the least documented. Establish what exists
for Azerbaijani and Turkish diaspora communities first, then Georgian, Armenian, Central Asian, South
Asian and Southeast Asian ones — community funds of any faith or none, and secular associations —
and whether any publishes an application route a student could follow.

### Sourcing rules

1. **Primary sources for load-bearing claims** — the funder's own page or its filed report.
   Scholarship-aggregator sites may be used to find a scheme, never to establish a fact about it.
   This family is *heavily* polluted by SEO content farms republishing dead schemes.
2. **Confirm the scheme is currently open.** Give the most recent cycle you can evidence and its
   date. Corporate schemes are quietly discontinued more often than government ones, and a dead
   scheme presented as live is worse than no result.
3. **Quote the operative sentence** for eligibility, application route, non-financial conditions
   and any bonding clause.
4. **Where a fact is unpublished, say so.** Do not infer coverage amounts, applicant numbers or
   eligibility from a scheme's marketing language.
5. Where sources conflict, give both with dates.

### What would change our plans

Flag explicitly: any structured, openly-licensed directory of corporate or foundation *schemes*; any
`DIRECT`-route full-cost scheme open to our nationalities; any country whose CSR disclosure regime
makes scholarship schemes systematically enumerable; and any university that maintains a genuinely
comprehensive list of external partner scholarships, which would be reusable as a supplier.

### Floor — check these only after Part 0 is published

These are placed last so they cannot anchor the enumeration. They are a floor on coverage, never a
ceiling, and **none is a finding by appearing here**: verify each at its own page, and report it only
if your own evidence supports it. If your Part 0 did not reach one of them, say so — that is a
measurement of the enumeration, not a failure to hide.

- Mastercard Foundation Scholars and Rotary grants — believed to be nominated routes; confirm or
  refute from the funder's own text.
- Germany's party-affiliated *Stiftungen* (e.g. Konrad-Adenauer, Heinrich-Böll, Friedrich-Ebert) —
  believed to fund some international master's students, with political-alignment and language
  conditions. Establish amounts and conditions yourself; check whether other countries' parties or
  unions run equivalents.
- The Aga Khan Foundation International Scholarship Programme page, which names other funders — one
  network's list among many, to be treated as tip-offs.
