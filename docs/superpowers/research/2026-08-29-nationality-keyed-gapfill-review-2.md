# **The Architecture of Master's-Level Human Capital Funding: An Analysis of the Eurasian and Transcaucasian Corridors**

## **Structural Derivation of the Funder Landscape**

The global landscape of master’s-level educational funding operates not as a decentralized marketplace of open grants, but as a highly regulated matrix of bilateral diplomacy, intergovernmental treaty obligations, and geopolitical soft-power initiatives. Traditional approaches to identifying educational funding rely on searching for pre-existing scheme names, a methodological flaw that inevitably overlooks localized or emergent funding corridors. To circumvent this, the present analysis structurally derives the funding landscape from first principles, focusing on the target populations of Azerbaijan, Türkiye, Georgia, Armenia, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Turkmenistan. This derivation is executed by mapping intergovernmental memberships, analyzing affinity-based capital flows, and examining origin-side state curation.  
The availability of international capacity-building funds is inextricably linked to a nation’s integration into global and regional multilateral frameworks. When a state acts as a member, observer, associate, or dialogue partner, its citizens frequently gain access to developmental funds embedded within those treaties. For Türkiye and Azerbaijan, their geopolitical positioning bridges European, Islamic, and Turkic spheres, unlocking distinct and occasionally overlapping funding corridors. The structural reality is that funding follows geopolitical alignment. Consequently, destination governments deploy soft power through educational capital (such as the European Union’s Eastern Partnership for the South Caucasus or Japan’s Official Development Assistance mechanisms), while development banks target specific macroeconomic and socioeconomic indicators inherent to their borrowing members.  
Beyond intergovernmental bodies, domestic origin-side infrastructure curates and controls these outflows. In Azerbaijan, the State Agency for Science and Higher Education and the Ministry of Science and Education act as centralized clearinghouses, actively publicizing and managing foreign state scholarship opportunities. In Türkiye, while the Presidency for Turks Abroad and Related Communities (YTB) functions primarily as an inbound funder, the broader outbound landscape is managed through a complex web of bilateral agreements coordinated by entities such as the Directorate for EU Affairs and non-governmental philanthropic partners. This dynamic similarly plays out across the Caucasus and Central Asia, where entities like Georgia’s International Education Center and Armenia’s Ministry of Education, Science, Culture and Sport transform open international applications into state-managed diplomatic quotas.

### **Verification of the Suspected Ecosystem**

An in-depth architectural review of the suspected funding bodies reveals critical nuances regarding their operational mechanisms, eligibility constraints, and the specific nature of their capital deployment.  
The Joint Japan/World Bank Graduate Scholarship Program (JJ/WBGSP) operates not as an open academic grant, but as a highly structured, mid-career capacity-building tool. The scheme is strictly restricted to citizens who are a "national of a World Bank member developing country"13. The architecture of the application process is rigidly divided into Window 1 and Window 2, mandating that applicants hold a bachelor's degree earned at least three years prior to the application deadline and possess three years of development-related employment13. Furthermore, the scheme dictates that scholars must enroll in one of "44 Participating Master's Programs in 24 universities in the U.S., Europe, Africa, Oceania and Japan," specifically in key areas of development such as economic policy management, tax policy, and infrastructure management13. This prevents the arbitrary selection of degree programs by the applicant, ensuring that the human capital generated directly aligns with World Bank institutional priorities.  
The Swedish Institute (SI) Scholarships for Global Professionals explicitly utilizes geographic eligibility to advance Sweden's foreign policy and international development cooperation goals. The scheme is highly lucrative, providing full tuition fee coverage, a monthly allowance of SEK 12,000 for living expenses, and a travel grant of up to SEK 15,00012. However, eligibility is hyper-specific. Among the target populations, only citizens of Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia, and Uzbekistan are eligible12. The Swedish Institute enforces a strict professional experience requirement, but structurally asymmetric conditions apply depending on the applicant's origin. Applicants from Uzbekistan must possess a minimum of 3,000 hours of documented work experience12. Conversely, applicants from Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Georgia must provide proof of work experience, but "there is no requirement of number of hours, however applicants with more experience will be prioritised"12. Crucially, Türkiye, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Turkmenistan are excluded from the eligibility list for this specific scheme, demonstrating the precise geographic targeting of Swedish state funds12.  
The Baltic states have aggressively positioned themselves as educational hubs for the post-Soviet and Transcaucasian spaces, leveraging historical ties, affordable living costs, and English-taught degree programs to project regional influence. In Latvia, the State Education Development Agency (VIAA) administers a robust Master's scholarship program. The results published on 29 June 2026 demonstrate heavy, deliberate allocation to the target region. Out of 104 total applicants awarded scholarships globally, Azerbaijan received 10, Uzbekistan received 9, and Georgia received 61. This concentration indicates a clear strategic preference for Eurasian integration. Similarly, Lithuania's Education Exchanges Support Foundation operates state scholarships for full-time Master's degrees. The operational mechanism here differs slightly, requiring applicants to first independently apply to a Lithuanian higher education institution and secure admission or pre-acceptance before they are permitted to submit the state scholarship application through the centralized governmental system15.  
Erasmus+ International Credit Mobility (ICM), specifically categorized under Key Action 171 (KA171), is widely misunderstood in popular discourse as an individual, open-market scholarship. Architecturally, it is an institutional grant mechanism. Students cannot apply directly to the European Union or the Erasmus+ program17. Instead, they must be selected by their home university, which acts as the primary gatekeeper. This bilateral model requires a European university and a non-EU partner institution to sign an Inter-Institutional Agreement (IIA) funded by a project grant awarded by a national Erasmus+ agency17. The scheme funds short-term master's mobility ranging from 2 to 12 months, providing a monthly individual support stipend and a travel grant calculated by distance bands (e.g., up to €1,735 for travel exceeding 8,000 km)17. Because the selection process is outsourced to the origin institution, the evaluation criteria reflect local academic priorities. For example, documented selection rubrics from participating non-EU institutions demonstrate a scoring matrix heavily weighting the applicant's GPA (60 points), language knowledge (15 points), and lack of previous mobility (30 points)19.  
An investigation into the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD) establishes a confirmed absence of a master's degree scholarship scheme. The EBRD operates the International Professionals Programme (IPP)9. This is an employment initiative, defined as a 24-month immersive early-career track that requires applicants to already possess a recent degree in a relevant field and fluency in English9. While the EBRD finances extensive e-learning modules—such as the Trade Finance e-Learning Programme, which trained over 5,000 students across 185 banks in 36 countries—it does not finance outbound master's degrees for citizens of its countries of operation20.  
Similarly, research into UNESCO yields a confirmed absence for an open graduate academic scholarship. UNESCO operates the Young Professionals Programme (YPP), which requires an "advanced university degree" and functions as a recruitment initiative for employment at the P-1 and P-2 professional levels11. Eligibility is strictly limited to nationals from non- and under-represented Member States who are under 32 years of age11. While specialized branches like UNESCO-UNEVOC operate a TVET Leadership Programme for mid-to-senior professionals21, and UNESCO-IBE partners with universities to implement Master's or Postgraduate Diplomas in Curriculum design10, these are institutional capacity-building partnerships and leadership courses, not direct financial grants awarded to the general public for independent master's study. The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) mirrors this posture, operating through project-based capacity building and professional fellowships rather than acting as a traditional academic grant-making body.

## **Verification of a Contested Absence: The ADB-JSP Architecture**

The Asian Development Bank-Japan Scholarship Program (ADB-JSP) was established in April 1988 with financing from the Government of Japan22. The program was designed to provide an opportunity for well-qualified citizens of the ADB's developing member countries to undertake graduate studies in economics, management, science and technology, and other development-related fields22. A rigorous analysis of the ADB's official, primary-source eligibility criteria clarifies the contested absence regarding specific nations, particularly the status of Türkiye.  
The operative rule for ADB-JSP eligibility is dual-layered and non-negotiable. To qualify, an applicant must "be a national of an ADB borrowing member and Japanese ODA scholarship eligible country"22. Furthermore, the regulations explicitly state that applicants from countries that are "no longer borrowing from ADB are not eligible" for the scholarship26.  
The official list of eligible nations is highly specific. It includes Afghanistan, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, Cook Islands, Federated States of Micronesia, Fiji, Georgia, India, Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Kiribati, Kyrgyz Republic, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Maldives, Marshall Islands, Mongolia, Myanmar, Nauru, Nepal, Niue, Pakistan, Palau, Papua New Guinea, Philippines, Samoa, Solomon Islands, Sri Lanka, Tajikistan, Thailand, Timor-Leste, Tonga, Turkmenistan, Tuvalu, Uzbekistan, Vanuatu, and Viet Nam5.  
**Major Discovery:** Empirical review of this official eligibility list confirms that Türkiye is completely excluded from the ADB-JSP. It does not appear on the dual-eligibility list of borrowing members and Japanese ODA recipients, thereby confirming its absence from this funding corridor5.  
The application route for the ADB-JSP represents a highly decentralized architectural model. The ADB explicitly dictates that "no separate application to ADB is necessary"29. Instead, applicants must independently request information and apply directly to one of the approximately 25 designated academic institutions (DIs) located across 9 countries, which include prominent universities such as Hitotsubashi University, Keio University, the University of Tokyo, and the Asian Institute of Technology22. During this institutional application, the candidate must indicate their intent to be considered for the ADB-JSP and submit all required documentation at least six months prior to the planned commencement of studies5. The designated academic institution assumes the burden of screening and ranking the applicants based on its own selection criteria while keeping the ADB's eligibility requirements in view. The institution then submits a ranked list of candidates to the ADB, which subsequently recommends awardees to the Executive Director of Japan for final formal approval22.  
To enforce its developmental mandate, the ADB imposes strict demographic, professional, and socioeconomic caps on its coverage. The scholarship provides full tuition fees, a monthly subsistence and housing allowance (for example, approximately 144,000 JPY at Ritsumeikan Asia Pacific University), an allowance for books and instructional materials, comprehensive medical insurance, and economy class travel expenses between the home country and the institution22. However, to access these funds, applicants must not hold dual citizenship with any developed country, must not already be enrolled in a graduate degree program, and must not be more than 35 years old at the time of application (unless exceptional waivers are granted by the Government of Japan)22.  
Professionally, the program enforces a strict capacity-building philosophy by mandating that candidates possess at least two years of full-time professional working experience acquired after obtaining a university degree22. Socioeconomically, the program is heavily means-tested. The applicant's family income must not exceed USD 50,000 per year, and the applicant's individual income must not exceed USD 25,000 per year, which must be rigorously verified through local municipality-certified income tax returns or official salary certifications28. Finally, to prevent human capital flight, the recipient must sign an agreement committing to return to their home country for a period of at least two years after the completion of their studies to contribute to its socioeconomic development22.

## **Nationality-Keyed Tracks and the Illusion of Global Competition**

Major sovereign-funded scholarship schemes frequently mask highly stratified, bilateral quotas beneath a facade of global, open competition. While these programs advertise thousands of available scholarships worldwide, the internal reality is that opportunities are heavily gated by nationality to advance specific geopolitical, economic, and diplomatic objectives of the funding state.

### **Quota Allocation in the Global Korea Scholarship**

The South Korean government, operating through the National Institute for International Education (NIIED), manages the Global Korea Scholarship (GKS) through a distinct dual-track architecture: the University Track and the Embassy Track. The University Track allows applicants to apply directly to Korean higher education institutions, fostering institutional autonomy and driving international enrollment to regional universities. Conversely, the Embassy Track operates as a direct instrument of bilateral statecraft. It establishes hard, non-negotiable quotas for specific partner nations to advance Seoul's geopolitical footprint, allowing local Korean embassies to pre-screen and interview candidates.  
In 2024, the GKS Graduate program established a global quota of 800 scholars through the Embassy Track32. In 2025, the total quota was adjusted to 1,820 students, with 720 allocated to the Embassy Track and 1,100 to the University Track33. For the target populations within this analysis, the Embassy Track quotas are explicitly keyed by nationality, revealing a strategic prioritization of specific Central Asian and Transcaucasian states.

### **Geographic Filtering in the DAAD and Dynamic Reallocation in Türkiye/China**

The German Academic Exchange Service (DAAD) operates the "Study Scholarships \- Master Studies for All Academic Disciplines." Unlike the GKS, which publishes hard mathematical quotas globally, the DAAD utilizes opaque geographic filtering at the point of application entry. The official DAAD portal explicitly restricts this specific master's program, noting that the scholarship is restricted to "Only specified" countries37. Structural analysis confirms that Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia, and Kazakhstan are actively included on the authorized list of eligible nations for this specific master's scholarship track37.  
Türkiye Bursları and the Chinese Government Scholarship (CGS) utilize a highly opaque, dynamically re-allocated regional quota system. While global macroeconomic targets are announced—with Türkiye Bursları historically granting upwards of 4,000 scholarships annually across all academic levels—per-country master's quotas are rarely codified in public, static documents. Instead, these quotas are negotiated bilaterally behind closed doors and adjusted annually based on shifting diplomatic priorities. For China, these quotas align tightly with Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) logistics nodes and resource extraction partnerships in Central Asia. For Türkiye, the allocation heavily prioritizes the "Turkic World" (Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Turkmenistan) to build strategic depth and cultural hegemony in the region, operating alongside institutions like the Organization of Turkic States.

### **The Stipendium Hungaricum Quota Asymmetry**

The Stipendium Hungaricum (SH) is the cornerstone of Hungary's "Eastern Opening" foreign policy, designed to foster economic and cultural ties with emerging markets. A major unresolved question in scholarship analysis has been the publication of per-country quotas for this scheme. A meticulous examination of the official national education agency websites of Azerbaijan (State Agency for Science and Higher Education), Georgia (International Education Center), and Armenia (Ministry of Education, Science, Culture and Sport) reveals a distinct and deliberate structural asymmetry: **Hungary does not publish sending-side quotas centrally; instead, the sending states independently publish the quotas negotiated in their respective bilateral Memoranda of Understanding. The funder does not publish it—that asymmetry is itself the finding.**  
This asymmetric publication is a deliberate architectural choice by the Hungarian government. By delegating the quota management, primary vetting, and domestic advertisement to the origin countries, Hungary outsources the administrative burden and ensures that the incoming cohorts align with the diplomatic and economic priorities of the partner state, thereby securing political goodwill.  
In Georgia, the International Education Center (IEC) publishes the terms of the SH agreement and actively leverages it for domestic policy. The IEC operates a "State-Funded Support Program for 'Stipendium Hungaricum' Scholarship Holders with High Academic Performance" for the 2025–2026 and 2026-2027 academic years39. Georgian recipients who have successfully completed at least their first semester in Hungary and maintained a minimum GPA of 4.0 (under Hungary's 5-point grading system) receive an additional grant of €1,250 per semester directly from the Georgian state to cover living expenses41. To receive this supplementary funding, the IEC enforces a strict national return mandate, dictating that scholars must "return to Georgia no later than one month after completing their studies, and reside and be employed in a relevant field for at least 3 years within 5 years of graduation"41.  
In Armenia, the Ministry of Education, Science, Culture and Sport (ESCS) routinely publishes the operationalization of the SH quotas to its domestic audience. The ESCS publicly announced the signing of Memoranda of Understanding on cooperation within the Stipendium Hungaricum scholarship program for the 2023-2025 period42, and subsequently formalized the agreement for the 2026-2028 period43. By controlling the narrative and the publication of the quota, the Armenian state claims ownership of the mobility opportunity to its citizens, presenting it as a dividend of successful state diplomacy.

## **The Turkish Outbound Master's Ecosystem (Excluding YLSY)**

Excluding the heavily analyzed YLSY (the primary state-sponsored study abroad program governed by Law No. 1416), the outbound funding landscape for Turkish citizens seeking master's degrees is characterized by stringent post-study return mandates and a heavy reliance on bilateral agreements and private philanthropic foundations partnered with foreign states.

### **The Strategic Absence in Sovereign Research Funding (TÜBİTAK)**

A critical and defining discovery in the Turkish outbound funding ecosystem is the absolute absence of general master's degree funding by TÜBİTAK (The Scientific and Technological Research Council of Türkiye). TÜBİTAK’s primary overseas fellowship mechanisms are strictly reserved for advanced, post-graduate research.  
The 2213-A (Yurt Dışı Doktora Burs Programı) is the flagship outbound degree program. However, official documentation explicitly prohibits master's funding, mandating: "Program kapsamında sadece yurt dışı doktora bursu verilmektedir" (Under the scope of the program, only overseas doctoral scholarships are provided)7. Furthermore, the 2214-A program is designed exclusively for research during an ongoing PhD44, and the 2219 program supports post-doctoral research, providing a monthly stipend and round-trip airfare for scholars45.  
This structural absence delineates a clear strategic bifurcation in Turkish human capital policy. The state utilizes YLSY for directed, civil-service-bound master's-level capacity building, and outsources general master's mobility to bilateral partners and philanthropic entities. Sovereign research capital managed by TÜBİTAK is strictly preserved for advanced doctoral and postdoctoral knowledge production, ensuring that state scientific funds are not expended on foundational graduate coursework.

### **Bilateral Integration: The Jean Monnet Scholarship Program**

The Jean Monnet Scholarship Program is the premier outbound master's funding vehicle for Turkish citizens. Funded entirely by the European Union, the program is a direct instrument of structural integration, designed to support Türkiye's EU harmonization efforts and build bureaucratic capacity regarding the EU acquis.  
The eligibility parameters are exceptionally broad across the professional spectrum, open to public sector employees, private sector employees (including NGOs), university academic and administrative staff, and senior undergraduate or graduate students46. However, the academic constraints are rigid: the proposed master's education plan must strictly align with the EU acquis47.  
The scale of the program is significant. For the 2026-2027 academic year, the program targeted approximately 150 scholars, with applications closing on 4 November 202546. Funding amounts range from €700 to €22,000 depending on the specific European university program47. Demonstrating the continuous nature of this diplomatic tool, applications for the 2027-2028 academic year have already commenced, requiring applicants to undergo a rigorous selection process including a written examination46.

### **Soft Power Parity: The IsDB-Türkiye Joint Scholarship**

The Islamic Development Bank (IsDB) and Türkiye Scholarships (YTB) operate a joint program designed to support students from IsDB member countries and Muslim communities in non-member countries3. While this functions primarily as an inbound mechanism bringing students *to* Turkish universities, it highlights the sophisticated intertwining of Turkish state agencies with multilateral development banks to project soft power. The program focuses heavily on sustainability sciences to empower communities, providing tuition fees, living allowances, accommodation, health insurance, and round-trip airfare3. By co-funding this initiative, Türkiye elevates its status from a passive recipient of international development funds to an active benefactor and educational hub within the Islamic world.

### **Philanthropic Capital and the Mitigation of Brain Drain: Türk Eğitim Vakfı (TEV)**

In the absence of open state funding for general master's degrees, the Türk Eğitim Vakfı (TEV) functions as the primary non-governmental engine for Turkish outbound master's mobility, operating through strategic co-funding partnerships with foreign states and independent mechanisms.  
Since 1998, TEV has partnered with the German Academic Exchange Service (DAAD) to co-fund master's degrees in Germany for Turkish citizens across all academic disciplines, with the explicit exception of Medicine and Performing Arts50. The financial architecture of this partnership is an exact parity model: the living expenses and scholarship costs (averaging €25,000 over a two-year period) are split 50/50 between TEV and DAAD50. Approximately 45 new scholars are selected annually through this highly competitive track, which requires candidates to be under 35 years of age, hold a minimum GPA of 3.0/4.0 (or 76.5/100), and demonstrate stringent English or German language proficiency51. The application window for the 2026 cycle spans from 6 October 2025 to 30 January 202651.  
TEV also independently manages an outbound master's scholarship track for the United States and Western European nations. Unlike the DAAD partnership, this track is not a full-ride scholarship. It explicitly does not cover tuition fees; instead, it provides a contribution towards living expenses, allocating approximately USD 10,000 per scholar for the entirety of the master's program52. Applications for the 2026-2027 cycle in this track close on 5 March 202652.  
A defining, structural characteristic of TEV's outbound master's funding is its strict legal architecture designed to mitigate human capital flight. For scholars departing from the September-October 2024 academic cycle onwards, TEV imposes a rigorous contractual obligation backed by a financial guarantor (kefaletname). Upon completion of the funded master's degree (or a subsequent PhD, if authorized), the scholar is permitted to remain abroad for a maximum grace period of two years. Following this period, the scholar is legally mandated to return to Türkiye and remain resident in the country for a duration equal to *twice* the length of their funded master's program51. Failure to fulfill this strict residency requirement immediately converts the philanthropic scholarship into a financial debt, triggering a mandatory repayment obligation for the entire TEV-funded portion51. This mechanism ensures that private philanthropic capital deployed abroad translates directly into domestic economic capacity and knowledge transfer, effectively turning a potential brain drain into guaranteed brain circulation.
